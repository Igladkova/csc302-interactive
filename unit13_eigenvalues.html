1. Background: eigenvalues and eigenvectors (Heath §4.1–4.2)

\(A\mathbf x = \lambda\mathbf x\) with \(\mathbf x \ne \mathbf 0\): \(A\) only stretches \(\mathbf x\), by the factor \(\lambda\). The eigenvalues are the roots of \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\), a polynomial of degree \(n\). Facts used constantly:

  • \(\sum\lambda_i = \operatorname{trace}(A)\), \(\prod\lambda_i = \det A\); a triangular matrix has its diagonal entries as eigenvalues.
  • A real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors: \(A = Q\Lambda Q^T\).
  • Similar matrices \(B = T^{-1}AT\) have the same eigenvalues; algorithms transform \(A\) toward triangular or diagonal form by similarity (meeting 14).
  • Computing eigenvalues as polynomial roots is a bad idea: forming the coefficients is costly, and the roots can be extremely sensitive to rounding in them. For \(n \ge 5\) there is no formula anyway (Abel), so every eigenvalue method is iterative.

Example

Example 1. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\).

Answer

\(\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 6 = 0\) (trace 7, determinant 6), so \(\lambda_1 = 6\), \(\lambda_2 = 1\). For 6: \((A - 6I)\mathbf x = 0\) gives \(-x_1 + 2x_2 = 0\), \(\mathbf x_1 = (2, 1)\). For 1: \(4x_1 + 2x_2 = 0\), \(\mathbf x_2 = (1, -2)\). The eigenvectors are orthogonal, as they must be for a symmetric matrix.

2. Sensitivity and conditioning (Heath §4.3)

If \(A = X\Lambda X^{-1}\) is diagonalizable and \(E\) is a perturbation, every eigenvalue \(\mu\) of \(A + E\) satisfies (Bauer–Fike) \[ |\mu - \lambda_k| \le \operatorname{cond}_2(X)\,\|E\|_2 \quad \text{for some } k . \] For a single simple eigenvalue, the condition number is \(1/|\cos\theta|\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between its right eigenvector \(\mathbf x\) (\(A\mathbf x = \lambda\mathbf x\)) and left eigenvector \(\mathbf y\) (\(\mathbf y^TA = \lambda\mathbf y^T\)).

  • Symmetric (more generally, normal) matrices: \(X\) can be orthogonal, \(\operatorname{cond}_2(X) = 1\), so \(|\Delta\lambda| \le \|E\|_2\). Their eigenvalues are always well conditioned.
  • Nonsymmetric matrices with nearly parallel eigenvectors can have extremely sensitive eigenvalues, even when the eigenvalues are well separated.
  • Eigenvectors are sensitive when eigenvalues are close together, even for symmetric matrices.

Example

Example 2. Perturb the \((2, 1)\) entry of \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 100 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) by \(10^{-4}\). How far do the eigenvalues 1 and 2 move? Compare with the symmetric \(\operatorname{diag}(1, 2)\) perturbed by \(10^{-4}\) in both off-diagonal entries.

Answer

The eigenvalues of \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 100 \\ 10^{-4} & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) are \(0.9901\) and \(2.0099\): they move by \(0.0099\), about 100 times the perturbation. The right eigenvector for \(\lambda = 1\) is \((1, 0)\), the left one is nearly \((0.01, -1)\), so \(\cos\theta \approx 0.01\) and the condition number is about 100. Equivalently, \(\operatorname{cond}_2(X) = 200\). In the symmetric case the eigenvalues move by only \(10^{-8}\), well within the bound \(\|E\|_2 = 10^{-4}\).

3. Power iteration (Heath §4.5.1)

Repeat \(\mathbf y_k = A\mathbf x_{k-1}\), \(\mathbf x_k = \mathbf y_k/\|\mathbf y_k\|_\infty\). Expand \(\mathbf x_0 = \sum\alpha_j\mathbf v_j\) in eigenvectors: \(A^k\mathbf x_0 = \lambda_1^k\bigl(\alpha_1\mathbf v_1 + \sum_{j>1}(\lambda_j/\lambda_1)^k\alpha_j\mathbf v_j\bigr)\). So if \(|\lambda_1| > |\lambda_2| \ge \cdots\) and \(\alpha_1 \ne 0\), \(\mathbf x_k\) turns toward \(\mathbf v_1\) and \(\|\mathbf y_k\|_\infty \to |\lambda_1|\), linearly with ratio \(|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|\). The Rayleigh quotient \(\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x/\mathbf x^T\mathbf x\) is a better estimate: for symmetric \(A\) its error is the square of the eigenvector error. Each step costs one matrix–vector product, so power iteration is the method for huge sparse matrices that need only the dominant eigenvector, such as Google's PageRank.

Example

Example 3. Power iteration for \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) from \(\mathbf x_0 = (1, 0)\). Give the estimate \(\|\mathbf y_k\|_\infty\) and the Rayleigh quotient for 3 steps.

Answer

\(\mathbf y_1 = (5, 2)\), estimate 5, \(\mathbf x_1 = (1, 0.4)\); \(\mathbf y_2 = (5.8, 2.8)\), estimate 5.8, \(\mathbf x_2 = (1, 0.4828)\); \(\mathbf y_3 = (5.9655, 2.9655)\), estimate 5.9655, \(\mathbf x_3 = (1, 0.4971)\). Errors \(1, 0.2, 0.0345\): each step divides the error by \(\lambda_1/\lambda_2 = 6\). The Rayleigh quotients of \(\mathbf x_1, \mathbf x_2, \mathbf x_3\) are \(5.9655, 5.99904, 5.999973\): errors \(3.4\times10^{-2}, 9.6\times10^{-4}, 2.7\times10^{-5}\), about the square of the eigenvector error at each step. The figure shows \(\mathbf x_k\) turning toward \((2, 1)\).

4. Inverse iteration and shifts (Heath §4.5.2)

The eigenvalues of \((A - \sigma I)^{-1}\) are \(1/(\lambda_j - \sigma)\), and the largest of these comes from the \(\lambda_j\) closest to \(\sigma\). Power iteration on \((A - \sigma I)^{-1}\), where each step solves \((A - \sigma I)\mathbf y_k = \mathbf x_{k-1}\) with one LU factorization reused (meeting 9), converges to that eigenvector with ratio \[ \frac{|\lambda_{\text{closest}} - \sigma|}{|\lambda_{\text{next closest}} - \sigma|} . \] A good shift gives very fast convergence. Inverse iteration is the standard way to compute an eigenvector once an approximate eigenvalue is known. The near-singularity of \(A - \sigma I\) does no harm: the error it causes lies almost entirely along the wanted eigenvector.

Example

Example 4. Inverse iteration for the same \(A\) with shift \(\sigma = 1.5\), from \(\mathbf x_0 = (1, 0)\). Which eigenvalue, and at what rate?

Answer

It finds \(\lambda = 1\), the eigenvalue closest to 1.5, with ratio \(|1 - 1.5|/|6 - 1.5| = 0.111\). Iterates (normalized): \((-0.25, 1)\), \((-0.531, 1)\), \((-0.4966, 1)\), approaching \((-0.5, 1) \parallel (1, -2)\). Rayleigh quotients: \(1.235, 1.0030, 1.0000376\).

5. Rayleigh quotient iteration (Heath §4.5.3)

Use the current Rayleigh quotient as the shift in every step: \(\sigma_k = \mathbf x_k^TA\mathbf x_k/\mathbf x_k^T\mathbf x_k\), then solve \((A - \sigma_kI)\mathbf y_{k+1} = \mathbf x_k\). Convergence is quadratic in general and cubic for symmetric matrices, but each step needs a new factorization (\(n^3/3\)), and which eigenvalue it finds depends on the start.

Example

Example 5. Rayleigh quotient iteration for the same \(A\) from \(\mathbf x_0 = (1, 0)\).

Answer

Shifts: \(\sigma_0 = 5\), \(\sigma_1 = 5.923\), \(\sigma_2 = 5.999981\), \(\sigma_3 = 6\) to machine precision. Errors \(1, 7.7\times10^{-2}, 1.9\times10^{-5}, 0\): roughly cubed at each step. At the end \(A - \sigma I\) is singular to working precision; that signals convergence.

8. Try it, and review questions

Interactive: Power and inverse iteration. Lab 8 uses power iteration for PageRank.

  1. Why is computing eigenvalues as roots of the characteristic polynomial a bad numerical method?
  2. State the Bauer–Fike theorem. Why are the eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix always well conditioned?
  3. Give a \(2\times2\) matrix whose eigenvalues move much more than a small perturbation of its entries.
  4. Why does power iteration fail if \(\mathbf x_0\) has no component along \(\mathbf v_1\)? Why is that rarely a problem in floating point?
  5. A matrix has eigenvalues \(10, 9, 1\). How many power iterations reduce the error by \(10^{-6}\)? What shift would you use with inverse iteration to find 9 quickly?
  6. Why is it fine that \(A - \sigma I\) is nearly singular in inverse iteration?
  7. Why is the Rayleigh quotient a better eigenvalue estimate than the normalization factor?
  8. Compare the cost per step and the rate of inverse iteration and Rayleigh quotient iteration.
