Meeting 19 summary: systems of nonlinear equations, Newton and Broyden

Learning objectives

  • Picture a system \(\mathbf F(\mathbf x) = \mathbf 0\) of two equations as intersecting curves, and its solution's conditioning through the Jacobian.
  • Decide when a fixed-point iteration \(\mathbf x_{k+1} = \mathbf G(\mathbf x_k)\) converges, using the Jacobian of \(\mathbf G\).
  • Carry out Newton's method for systems by hand (\(2 \times 2\)): form \(\mathbf F\) and \(J\), solve \(J\mathbf s = -\mathbf F\), update; explain its quadratic convergence and its cost.
  • Describe Broyden's secant update and why it is cheaper per step but only superlinear.
  • Explain how damping (line search) and trust regions make Newton-like methods more robust far from the solution.

This page summarizes Heath §5.6 together with Stewart §14.4 (tangent planes, applied), CSc 302 meeting 19. It extends meeting 17 and meeting 18 from one equation to \(n\). Lab 10 applies it (GPS positioning, a robot arm).

1. Systems of nonlinear equations; conditioning (Heath §5.1–5.3, §5.6)

Find \(\mathbf x \in \mathbb R^n\) with \(\mathbf F(\mathbf x) = \mathbf 0\), that is, \(f_1(\mathbf x) = \cdots = f_n(\mathbf x) = 0\). For \(n = 2\), each equation \(f_i(x, y) = 0\) is a curve, the zero level curve of the surface \(z = f_i(x, y)\). The solutions are the points where the two curves cross.

  • Curves can cross many times, touch, or miss each other. There is no bracketing in two or more dimensions, so no analogue of bisection, and good starting guesses matter more.
  • The derivative becomes the Jacobian matrix \(J(\mathbf x)\), \(J_{ij} = \partial f_i / \partial x_j\). The solution is well conditioned when \(J(\mathbf x^*)\) is well conditioned: the curves cross at a healthy angle. If \(J(\mathbf x^*)\) is singular (for example, curves tangent to each other), the solution is infinitely sensitive, like a multiple root.

2. Fixed-point iteration for systems (Heath §5.6.1)

Rewrite the system as \(\mathbf x = \mathbf G(\mathbf x)\) and iterate. The one-dimensional condition \(|g'(x^*)| < 1\) becomes a condition on the Jacobian \(G'(\mathbf x^*)\) of \(\mathbf G\): the iteration converges locally if its spectral radius \(\rho(G'(\mathbf x^*)) < 1\) (largest eigenvalue modulus). It is enough to check \(\|G'(\mathbf x^*)\| < 1\) in any norm, since \(\rho \le \|\cdot\|\). The convergence is linear with rate \(\rho\), and quadratic if \(G'(\mathbf x^*) = 0\), as for Newton.

Example

Example 1. Does \(x_{k+1} = \tfrac12\cos y_k\), \(y_{k+1} = \tfrac12 \sin x_k\) converge, and how fast?

Answer

\(G'(x, y) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -\tfrac12\sin y \\ \tfrac12\cos x & 0\end{bmatrix}\). Every entry has magnitude at most \(\tfrac12\), so \(\|G'\|_\infty \le \tfrac12 < 1\) everywhere: it converges from any start. From \((0, 0)\) it reaches \(\mathbf x^* = (0.48641, 0.23373)\), where \(\rho(G'(\mathbf x^*)) = 0.226\). The errors 0.234, 0.0149, 0.0060, 0.00077, 0.00031, … shrink by about \(\rho^2 \approx 0.05\) every two steps.

3. Newton's method for systems (Heath §5.6.2; Stewart §14.4)

Replace each \(f_i\) by its linearization at \(\mathbf x_k\), the tangent plane of the surface \(z = f_i(x, y)\) (see the §14.4–14.6 review): \[ \mathbf F(\mathbf x_k + \mathbf s) \approx \mathbf F(\mathbf x_k) + J(\mathbf x_k)\,\mathbf s . \] Setting this to zero gives the Newton step: \[ J(\mathbf x_k)\,\mathbf s_k = -\mathbf F(\mathbf x_k), \qquad \mathbf x_{k+1} = \mathbf x_k + \mathbf s_k . \] Geometrically, intersect the zero lines of the tangent planes instead of the curves themselves.

  • Rate: quadratic near a solution where \(J(\mathbf x^*)\) is nonsingular, the \(n\)-dimensional version of meeting 18.
  • Cost per step: about \(n^2\) partial derivatives (or \(n\) extra evaluations of \(\mathbf F\) for a finite-difference Jacobian), plus an LU solve of about \(n^3/3\) operations (meeting 9). Never compute \(J^{-1}\).
  • Only local: far from the solution the linear model can be poor and the step can be huge (Section 5).

Examples

Example 2. Find an intersection of the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\) and the cubic \(y = x^3\): do one Newton step by hand from \((1, 1)\), then list the errors.

Answer

\(\mathbf F = \begin{bmatrix} x^2 + y^2 - 1 \\ y - x^3 \end{bmatrix}\), \(J = \begin{bmatrix} 2x & 2y \\ -3x^2 & 1\end{bmatrix}\). At \((1, 1)\): \(\mathbf F = (1, 0)\), \(J = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ -3 & 1\end{bmatrix}\), \(\det J = 8\). \(\mathbf s = J^{-1}(-\mathbf F) = \tfrac18\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 3 & 2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = (-0.125, -0.375)\), so \(\mathbf x_1 = (0.875, 0.625)\). Continuing: \((0.82904, 0.56435)\), \((0.82604, 0.56362)\), …; the solution is \((0.82603, 0.56362)\). Errors 0.0785, \(3.1\times10^{-3}\), \(9.8\times10^{-6}\), \(8.4\times10^{-11}\): quadratic. \(\operatorname{cond}(J(\mathbf x^*)) = 1.75\), so this solution is well conditioned.

4. Broyden's method: secant updating (Heath §5.6.3)

Avoid recomputing \(J\) every step: keep an approximation \(B_k\), solve \(B_k \mathbf s_k = -\mathbf F(\mathbf x_k)\), and update it with the smallest change that satisfies the secant equation \(B_{k+1}\mathbf s_k = \mathbf y_k\), where \(\mathbf y_k = \mathbf F(\mathbf x_{k+1}) - \mathbf F(\mathbf x_k)\): \[ B_{k+1} = B_k + \frac{(\mathbf y_k - B_k \mathbf s_k)\,\mathbf s_k^{T}}{\mathbf s_k^{T}\mathbf s_k} . \]

  • A rank-one update: no derivatives after \(B_0\) (often \(J(\mathbf x_0)\) or a finite-difference estimate), and the factorization can be updated in \(O(n^2)\) instead of refactoring in \(O(n^3)\).
  • Convergence is superlinear, not quadratic. It takes more steps but each is cheaper: the secant method of meeting 18 in \(n\) dimensions.

Example

Example 3. Run Broyden on the system of Example 2 from \((1, 1)\) with \(B_0 = J(1, 1)\).

Answer

The first step equals Newton's, \(\mathbf x_1 = (0.875, 0.625)\), and then \(B_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1.875 & 1.625 \\ -2.964 & 1.108\end{bmatrix}\) (the true \(J(\mathbf x_1)\) is \(\begin{bmatrix} 1.75 & 1.25 \\ -2.297 & 1\end{bmatrix}\)). Errors: 0.0785, 0.0147, 0.0024, \(7.1\times10^{-4}\), \(1.7\times10^{-5}\), \(7.9\times10^{-8}\), \(4.8\times10^{-11}\): superlinear, three more steps than Newton but with no Jacobian evaluations after the first.

5. Robust Newton-like methods (Heath §5.6.4)

  • Damped Newton (line search): compute the Newton step \(\mathbf s_k\) but take \(\mathbf x_{k+1} = \mathbf x_k + \alpha_k \mathbf s_k\), halving \(\alpha_k\) from 1 until \(\|\mathbf F\|\) decreases. Near the solution \(\alpha_k = 1\) and quadratic convergence returns.
  • Trust region: limit the step to a region where the linear model is trusted, and enlarge or shrink the region depending on how well the model predicted the actual decrease.
  • Library solvers (scipy.optimize.root, fsolve) combine these ideas with finite-difference or Broyden Jacobians.

Example

Example 4. For the system of Example 2, start at \((0.1, 0.1)\). The full Newton step goes to \((4.95, 0.147)\). What does damping do?

Answer

\(\|\mathbf F\|\) is 0.985 at the start but 124 after the full step: the first equation is almost flat at the start (its gradient, the first row of \(J = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 & 0.2 \\ -0.03 & 1\end{bmatrix}\), is small), so its tangent plane meets zero far away, where the linear model is useless. Halving: \(\alpha = \tfrac12\) gives 16.9, \(\tfrac14\) gives 2.28, \(\tfrac18\) gives 0.548. Accept \(\alpha = \tfrac18\) and continue from there.
