Learning objectives

  • Write the Taylor series of a function about a point a, and recognize the standard Maclaurin series.
  • Build the Taylor polynomial \(T_n\) and use it to approximate a function value.
  • Bound the remainder \(R_n = f - T_n\) with Taylor's Inequality or the Alternating Series Estimation Theorem.
  • Predict how the error changes when the distance \(h = |x - a|\) or the degree \(n\) changes.

This page reviews material from Calculus II (Stewart §11.10–11.11) that CSc 302 uses from meeting 1 on. Read it before the first class.

1. Taylor series

If \(f\) has a power series representation \(f(x) = \sum_{n\ge 0} c_n (x-a)^n\) for \(|x - a| < R\), its coefficients are forced to be \[ c_n = \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!} \qquad \text{(Stewart, Theorem 11.10.5)}, \] so the series must be the Taylor series of \(f\) at \(a\): \[ f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2 + \frac{f'''(a)}{3!}(x-a)^3 + \cdots \] With \(a = 0\) it is called a Maclaurin series. Recall \(0! = 1\) and \(f^{(0)} = f\).

A Taylor series need not converge to \(f\). It does exactly when the remainder (Section 2) tends to zero as \(n \to \infty\) (Stewart, Theorem 11.10.8).

New series can be made from old ones by substitution, multiplication by a power of \(x\), and term-by-term differentiation or integration. Because the power series of a function is unique, any of these routes gives the Taylor series.

Examples

Example 1. Find the Taylor series of \(f(x) = 1/x\) about \(a = 2\). For which \(x\) does it converge?

Answer

The derivatives follow a pattern: \(f^{(n)}(x) = (-1)^n\, n!\, x^{-(n+1)}\), so \(f^{(n)}(2)/n! = (-1)^n / 2^{n+1}\) and \[ \frac1x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{2^{n+1}}(x-2)^n = \frac12 - \frac{x-2}{4} + \frac{(x-2)^2}{8} - \cdots \] This is a geometric series with ratio \(-(x-2)/2\), so it converges for \(|x - 2| < 2\), that is, \(0 < x < 4\).

Example 2. Find the Maclaurin series of \(g(x) = x e^{-x^2}\) without computing any derivatives.

Answer

Replace \(x\) by \(-x^2\) in the series for \(e^x\), then multiply by \(x\): \[ x e^{-x^2} = x\sum_{n\ge0} \frac{(-x^2)^n}{n!} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n x^{2n+1}}{n!} = x - x^3 + \frac{x^5}{2} - \frac{x^7}{6} + \cdots \] It converges for all \(x\). (Check: at \(x = 0.5\) the first three terms give 0.390625, and \(0.5\,e^{-0.25} = 0.389400\).)

2. Taylor polynomials and the remainder

Stopping after the degree-\(n\) term gives the Taylor polynomial and the remainder \[ T_n(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(i)}(a)}{i!}(x-a)^i, \qquad R_n(x) = f(x) - T_n(x). \] \(T_1\) is the tangent line, \(T_2\) the best-fitting parabola, and so on: \(T_n\) matches \(f\) and its first \(n\) derivatives at \(a\). Dropping the remaining terms of the series is a truncation error, the first kind of error in this course.

How big is \(R_n\)?

  • Lagrange form (Stewart §11.10): \(R_n(x) = \dfrac{f^{(n+1)}(z)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}\) for some \(z\) between \(a\) and \(x\). The error looks like the next term of the series, with \(f^{(n+1)}\) evaluated at an unknown point.
  • Taylor's Inequality (Stewart, Theorem 11.10.9): if \(|f^{(n+1)}| \le M\) for \(|x - a| \le d\), then \[ |R_n(x)| \le \frac{M}{(n+1)!}\,|x-a|^{n+1} \quad \text{for } |x-a| \le d. \]
  • Alternating series (Stewart §11.11): if the series at \(x\) alternates and its terms decrease in size to 0, the error is smaller than the first omitted term.

How the error scales

For fixed \(n\) and \(h = |x - a| \to 0\), Taylor's Inequality says \(|R_n| \le C h^{n+1}\), written \(R_n = O(h^{n+1})\). So if the error is \(e_1\) at distance \(h_1\), then at distance \(h_2\) \[ e_2 \approx \left(\frac{h_2}{h_1}\right)^{n+1} e_1 . \] Halving \(h\) divides the error of \(T_n\) by about \(2^{n+1}\). On a log–log plot of error against \(h\) this is a straight line of slope \(n + 1\), a picture used all semester.

Examples

Example 3. Approximate \(e^{0.3}\) with \(T_3\) about \(a = 0\) and bound the error with Taylor's Inequality.

Answer

\(T_3(0.3) = 1 + 0.3 + \frac{0.09}{2} + \frac{0.027}{6} = 1.3495\). On \([0, 0.3]\), \(|f^{(4)}(x)| = e^x \le e^{0.3} < 1.35 = M\), so \[ |R_3(0.3)| \le \frac{1.35}{4!}(0.3)^4 = 4.56\times10^{-4}. \] The actual error is \(e^{0.3} - 1.3495 = 3.59\times10^{-4}\), inside the bound.

Example 4. How many terms of the Maclaurin series of \(\cos x\) guarantee \(|\cos(0.5) - T_n(0.5)| < 10^{-6}\)?

Answer

At \(x = 0.5\) the series alternates and its terms \(0.5^{2k}/(2k)!\) decrease: 1, 0.125, 0.0026, \(2.17\times10^{-5}\), \(9.69\times10^{-8}\), … The first term below \(10^{-6}\) is \(0.5^8/8!\), so it suffices to stop just before it: \(T_6(0.5) = 1 - \frac{0.25}{2} + \frac{0.0625}{24} - \frac{0.015625}{720} = 0.8775825\) (four nonzero terms). The actual error is \(9.7\times10^{-8}\).

Example 5. Use the binomial series with \(k = \tfrac12\) to estimate \(\sqrt{1.1}\) with two correction terms, and bound the error.

Answer

\(\sqrt{1+x} \approx 1 + \frac{x}{2} - \frac{x^2}{8}\), so \(\sqrt{1.1} \approx 1 + 0.05 - 0.00125 = 1.04875\). Here \(f'''(x) = \frac38 (1+x)^{-5/2} \le \frac38\) on \([0, 0.1]\), so \(|R_2(0.1)| \le \frac{3/8}{3!}(0.1)^3 = 6.25\times10^{-5}\). The actual error is \(5.88\times10^{-5}\).

Example 6. The error of \(T_2\) for \(e^x\) about 0 is \(1.40\times10^{-3}\) at \(h = 0.2\). Predict the error at \(h = 0.1\).

Answer

\(n = 2\), so the error is \(O(h^3)\): \(e_2 \approx (0.1/0.2)^3 \cdot 1.40\times10^{-3} = 1.75\times10^{-4}\). The actual value is \(1.71\times10^{-4}\). The prediction gets better as \(h \to 0\).

3. Why CSc 302 needs this

  • Truncation vs rounding. Every approximation in the course is a truncated series plus floating-point rounding. The two errors pull in opposite directions as \(h\) shrinks (meetings 1–3; Rounding vs truncation error; Lab 2).
  • Conditioning. \(T_1\) gives \(f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) \approx f'(x)\Delta x\), the source of the condition number \(|x f'(x)/f(x)|\) (meeting 2).
  • Several variables. The tangent plane and the multivariable Taylor expansion are the same idea in two variables (meeting 6; Tangent plane tab).
  • Newton's method. Each step replaces \(f\) by \(T_1\) and solves \(T_1(x) = 0\) (meetings 17–19; Newton's method tab).
  • Finite differences and quadrature. Error formulas such as \(\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = f'(x) + \frac h2 f''(z)\) come straight from \(T_1\) and the Lagrange remainder (meetings 5, 26–28; Finite differences tab).

Review questions

  1. Write the general formula for the Taylor series of \(f\) about \(a\). What does it reduce to when \(a = 0\)?
  2. Why is the Taylor series of a polynomial of degree \(m\) the polynomial itself, and what is \(R_n\) for \(n \ge m\)?
  3. Given \(f\), \(a\) and \(n\), can you write \(T_n\) and evaluate it?
  4. Can you get a new series (for example for \(e^{-x^2}\) or \(\int_0^x \sin t^2\,dt\)) from a table entry without computing derivatives?
  5. State Taylor's Inequality. How do you choose \(M\) for \(e^x\), \(\sin x\) and \(\sqrt{1+x}\) on a given interval?
  6. When can you use the Alternating Series Estimation Theorem instead, and what does it give?
  7. If the error of \(T_n\) at distance \(h\) is \(e\), what do you predict at distance \(h/2\)? On a log–log plot, what slope do you expect?
  8. How do you find the smallest \(n\) that guarantees an error below a given tolerance?
