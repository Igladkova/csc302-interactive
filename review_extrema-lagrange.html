1. Local extrema and the Second Derivatives Test (Stewart §14.7)

\(f\) has a local maximum at \((a, b)\) if \(f(x, y) \le f(a, b)\) for all \((x, y)\) near \((a, b)\), and a local minimum if \(f(x, y) \ge f(a, b)\) near \((a, b)\). If \(f\) has a local extremum at \((a, b)\) and its first partials exist there, then \(f_x(a, b) = f_y(a, b) = 0\), that is, \(\nabla f(a, b) = \mathbf 0\) (Stewart, Theorem 14.7.2). Such points, and points where a partial derivative does not exist, are critical points. Not every critical point is an extremum: at a saddle point \(f\) increases in some directions and decreases in others.

Second Derivatives Test. Let the second partials be continuous near a critical point \((a, b)\) and \[ D = f_{xx}(a, b)\,f_{yy}(a, b) - [f_{xy}(a, b)]^2 = \det \begin{bmatrix} f_{xx} & f_{xy} \\ f_{xy} & f_{yy} \end{bmatrix}. \]

  • \(D > 0\) and \(f_{xx} > 0\): local minimum.
  • \(D > 0\) and \(f_{xx} < 0\): local maximum.
  • \(D < 0\): saddle point.
  • \(D = 0\): no conclusion.

The matrix is the Hessian \(H\). In linear-algebra terms: \(H\) positive definite gives a minimum, negative definite a maximum, indefinite a saddle.

Absolute extrema on a closed, bounded region

A continuous \(f\) on a closed, bounded set \(D\) attains an absolute maximum and minimum (Extreme Value Theorem, Stewart 14.7.8). To find them (Stewart 14.7.9), compare:

  1. the values of \(f\) at the critical points inside \(D\);
  2. the extreme values of \(f\) on the boundary of \(D\), found with one-variable calculus on each piece or with Lagrange multipliers.

The largest of these values is the absolute maximum, and the smallest is the absolute minimum.

Examples

Example 1. Find and classify the critical points of \(f(x, y) = x^3 + y^3 - 3x - 12y\).

Answer

\(f_x = 3x^2 - 3 = 0\) gives \(x = \pm 1\); \(f_y = 3y^2 - 12 = 0\) gives \(y = \pm 2\): four critical points. \(f_{xx} = 6x\), \(f_{yy} = 6y\), \(f_{xy} = 0\), so \(D = 36xy\).

  • \((1, 2)\): \(D = 72 > 0\), \(f_{xx} = 6 > 0\): local minimum, \(f = -18\).
  • \((-1, -2)\): \(D = 72\), \(f_{xx} = -6\): local maximum, \(f = 18\).
  • \((1, -2)\) and \((-1, 2)\): \(D = -72 < 0\): saddle points, \(f = 14\) and \(-14\).

Example 2. Find the absolute maximum and minimum of \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - 2x\) on the closed disk \(x^2 + y^2 \le 4\).

Answer

Inside: \(\nabla f = \langle 2x - 2, 2y\rangle = \mathbf 0\) at \((1, 0)\), \(f = -1\). On the boundary \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\): \(f = 4 - 2x\) with \(-2 \le x \le 2\), so its values run from \(0\) at \((2, 0)\) to \(8\) at \((-2, 0)\). Comparing: absolute minimum \(-1\) at \((1, 0)\), absolute maximum \(8\) at \((-2, 0)\).

2. Lagrange multipliers (Stewart §14.8)

To find the extreme values of \(f(x, y)\) subject to a constraint \(g(x, y) = k\): at a constrained extremum the level curve of \(f\) is tangent to the constraint curve, so their normal vectors are parallel. Solve \[ \nabla f = \lambda \nabla g, \qquad g(x, y) = k \] for \(x, y, \lambda\): three equations, three unknowns (four of each for \(f(x, y, z)\)). Then evaluate \(f\) at all solutions; on a closed, bounded constraint set the largest value is the maximum and the smallest the minimum (Stewart, Method of Lagrange Multipliers).

  • The number \(\lambda\) is the Lagrange multiplier. It measures sensitivity: if the constraint level \(k\) changes, the optimal value changes at the rate \(df^*/dk = \lambda\).
  • Solve by cases. Divide only by quantities you know are nonzero, and treat the zero case separately.

Examples

Example 3. Find the extreme values of \(f(x, y) = x^2 y\) on the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 3\).

Answer

\(\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g\): \(2xy = 2\lambda x\), \(x^2 = 2\lambda y\). If \(x = 0\): \(y = \pm\sqrt3\), \(f = 0\). If \(x \ne 0\): \(\lambda = y\), so \(x^2 = 2y^2\), and the constraint gives \(3y^2 = 3\), \(y = \pm1\), \(x = \pm\sqrt2\). Values: \(f = 2\) at \((\pm\sqrt2, 1)\), \(f = -2\) at \((\pm\sqrt2, -1)\), \(f = 0\) at \((0, \pm\sqrt3)\). Maximum 2, minimum −2.

Example 4. Find the point of the plane \(x + 2y + 2z = 9\) closest to the origin, and use \(\lambda\) to estimate the new minimum of \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2\) if the plane becomes \(x + 2y + 2z = 9.3\).

Answer

Minimize \(f = x^2 + y^2 + z^2\): \(2x = \lambda\), \(2y = 2\lambda\), \(2z = 2\lambda\), so \(y = z = 2x\). Then \(x + 4x + 4x = 9\) gives \(x = 1\): the point \((1, 2, 2)\), \(f = 9\) (distance 3), \(\lambda = 2\). New level \(9.3\): \(f^* \approx 9 + 2(0.3) = 9.6\) (exact \(9.3^2/9 = 9.61\)).

3. Why CSc 302 needs this

  • \(\nabla f = \mathbf 0\) is a nonlinear system: Newton's method for it uses the Hessian as its Jacobian (meetings 19–22).
  • The Hessian test is the second-order optimality condition of Heath §6.2. Its eigenvalues decide the type of a critical point, and its condition number decides how hard the minimum is for steepest descent (meetings 20–22).
  • Lagrange's conditions are a nonlinear system in \((x, \lambda)\). Penalty methods and sequential quadratic programming solve it numerically (meeting 23).

Review questions

  1. What is a critical point? Give an example of a critical point that is not a local extremum.
  2. State the Second Derivatives Test. Why does \(D < 0\) mean a saddle?
  3. Classify the origin for \(x^2 + 4xy + y^2\) and for \(x^2 + y^4\). In which case does the test fail, and what is the answer?
  4. Why must the region be closed and bounded to guarantee absolute extrema? Give a counterexample when it is not.
  5. Describe the steps for finding absolute extrema on a closed rectangle.
  6. Explain geometrically why \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\) are parallel at a constrained extremum.
  7. Set up (do not solve) the Lagrange system for maximizing \(xyz\) subject to \(x + y + z = 12\).
  8. What does the value of \(\lambda\) tell you?
