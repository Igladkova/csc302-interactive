Learning objectives

  • Describe the general descent framework: a search direction plus a step length (line search).
  • Run steepest descent with an exact line search on a quadratic, and explain its zigzag and its dependence on the condition number of the Hessian.
  • Take a Newton step for minimization (\(H\mathbf s = -\nabla f\)), and explain its quadratic convergence, cost and safeguards.
  • Explain how BFGS builds a Hessian approximation from gradients, and why it is superlinear.
  • Explain why conjugate gradients minimizes an \(n\)-variable quadratic in \(n\) steps without storing a matrix.

This page summarizes Heath §6.5, CSc 302 meeting 22. It combines the optimality conditions of meeting 20, the line searches of meeting 21, and the Newton and Broyden ideas of meeting 19. Lab 11 compares these methods.

1. Descent methods; direct search (Heath §6.5–6.5.1)

Most methods for \(\min f(\mathbf x)\), \(\mathbf x \in \mathbb R^n\), repeat two steps:

  • choose a descent direction \(\mathbf s_k\), with \(\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)^T \mathbf s_k < 0\), so \(f\) decreases along it;
  • choose a step length \(\alpha_k\) by a line search, approximately minimizing \(\phi(\alpha) = f(\mathbf x_k + \alpha\mathbf s_k)\) (meeting 21), then set \(\mathbf x_{k+1} = \mathbf x_k + \alpha_k \mathbf s_k\).

The methods differ in how they choose \(\mathbf s_k\): \(-\nabla f\), a Newton step, a quasi-Newton step, or a conjugate direction. Direct search methods such as Nelder–Mead use only \(f\) values, comparing them at the corners of a simplex as golden section does. They are robust and need no derivatives, but are slow and impractical beyond a few variables.

2. Steepest descent (Heath §6.5.2)

Take \(\mathbf s_k = -\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)\), the direction of fastest local decrease (see the §14.4–14.6 review). It always makes progress while \(\nabla f \ne \mathbf 0\), but it is short-sighted. With an exact line search each new gradient is orthogonal to the previous step, so the path zigzags across narrow valleys.

  • On a quadratic \(f = \tfrac12\mathbf x^T A\mathbf x - \mathbf b^T\mathbf x\), with \(\mathbf g = \nabla f\), the exact step is \(\alpha = \dfrac{\mathbf g^T\mathbf g}{\mathbf g^T A\mathbf g}\).
  • Convergence is linear. In the worst case the error in \(f\) shrinks each step by about \(\left(\frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}\right)^2\), with \(\kappa = \operatorname{cond}(A) = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) of the Hessian. Elongated level sets (large \(\kappa\)) make it very slow.

Example

Example 1. Minimize \(f(x, y) = x^2 + 2xy + 6y^2\) (that is, \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 12\end{bmatrix}\), minimum at the origin) by steepest descent with exact line searches from \((-2, 1)\).

Answer

\(\mathbf g_0 = A\mathbf x_0 = (-2, 8)\), \(A\mathbf g_0 = (12, 92)\), so \(\alpha_0 = 68/712 = 0.0955\) and \(\mathbf x_1 = (-1.809, 0.236)\). Next \(\mathbf g_1 = (-3.146, -0.787)\), which is orthogonal to \(\mathbf g_0\), \(\alpha_1 = 0.2833\), \(\mathbf x_2 = (-0.918, 0.459)\). The values of \(f\): 6, 2.75, 1.26, 0.58, 0.27, … shrink by only 0.459 per step: after 10 steps the iterate is still 0.045 from the minimum. Here \(\kappa = 12.39/1.61 = 7.67\) and the worst-case factor \(\left(\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\right)^2\) is 0.592. The path zigzags down the narrow valley (figure).

3. Newton's method (Heath §6.5.3)

Minimize the quadratic Taylor model \(f(\mathbf x_k + \mathbf s) \approx f + \nabla f^T\mathbf s + \tfrac12\mathbf s^T H_f\mathbf s\): \[ H_f(\mathbf x_k)\,\mathbf s_k = -\nabla f(\mathbf x_k), \qquad \mathbf x_{k+1} = \mathbf x_k + \mathbf s_k . \] This is Newton's method for the system \(\nabla f = \mathbf 0\) (meeting 19); the Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient.

  • Quadratic convergence near a minimum with positive definite \(H_f(\mathbf x^*)\). On a quadratic it finishes in one step. The model also gives the step length, so no line search is needed near the solution.
  • Cost per step: about \(n^2/2\) second derivatives and a Cholesky solve (\(n^3/6\)), because \(H\) is symmetric and, near a minimum, positive definite.
  • Safeguards far from the minimum: if \(H_f(\mathbf x_k)\) is not positive definite, the Newton step may not even go downhill. Use a line search, a trust region, or a modified Hessian \(H + \mu I\).

Examples

Example 2. Newton's method for \(f(x, y) = e^{x+y} + x^2 + 3y^2\) from \((0, 0)\).

Answer

\(\nabla f = (e^{x+y} + 2x,\ e^{x+y} + 6y) = (1, 1)\) and \(H = \begin{bmatrix} e^{x+y} + 2 & e^{x+y} \\ e^{x+y} & e^{x+y} + 6\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 7\end{bmatrix}\) at the origin. \(\mathbf s = -\tfrac1{20}\begin{bmatrix} 7 & -1 \\ -1 & 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = (-0.3, -0.1)\). The minimum is at \((-0.32442, -0.10814)\), \(f = 0.78918\). Errors 0.342, 0.0257, \(1.3\times10^{-4}\), \(3.1\times10^{-9}\), \(10^{-17}\): quadratic. Steepest descent from the same start still has an error of \(1.4\times10^{-6}\) after 15 steps.

4. Quasi-Newton (secant updating) methods: BFGS (Heath §6.5.4–6.5.5)

Keep a symmetric positive definite approximation \(B_k\) of the Hessian. Solve \(B_k\mathbf s_k = -\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)\), line-search along \(\mathbf s_k\), and update with the BFGS formula. With \(\mathbf s_k\) now the actual step and \(\mathbf y_k = \nabla f(\mathbf x_{k+1}) - \nabla f(\mathbf x_k)\): \[ B_{k+1} = B_k + \frac{\mathbf y_k\mathbf y_k^T}{\mathbf y_k^T\mathbf s_k} - \frac{B_k\mathbf s_k\mathbf s_k^T B_k}{\mathbf s_k^T B_k\mathbf s_k} . \]

  • The update satisfies the secant equation \(B_{k+1}\mathbf s_k = \mathbf y_k\) (like Broyden, meeting 19), keeps \(B\) symmetric, and keeps it positive definite when \(\mathbf y_k^T\mathbf s_k > 0\).
  • Needs only gradients; with \(B_0 = I\) the first step is steepest descent. A factorization of \(B\) (or of \(B^{-1}\)) can be updated in \(O(n^2)\) work per step instead of \(O(n^3)\).
  • Superlinear convergence. BFGS is the default general-purpose method (scipy.optimize.minimize(method="BFGS")).

Example

Example 3. BFGS with \(B_0 = I\) and line searches for the function of Example 2, from \((0, 0)\).

Answer

Step 1 is steepest descent: \(\alpha_0 = 0.1759\), \(\mathbf x_1 = (-0.1759, -0.1759)\). Errors 0.342, 0.163, \(3.4\times10^{-3}\), \(2.1\times10^{-5}\), \(5.5\times10^{-9}\): superlinear. After six updates \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 2.650 & 0.649 \\ 0.649 & 6.649\end{bmatrix}\), close to the true Hessian at the minimum, \(\begin{bmatrix} 2.649 & 0.649 \\ 0.649 & 6.649\end{bmatrix}\), without a single second derivative. (The error then levels off near \(10^{-9}\), the line search's tolerance and the \(\sqrt\epsilon\) limit of meeting 20.) SciPy's BFGS needs 4 iterations and 21 function and gradient evaluations.

5. Conjugate gradients (Heath §6.5.6)

Instead of always going downhill along \(-\nabla f\), choose directions that are conjugate with respect to the Hessian, \(\mathbf s_i^T A\mathbf s_j = 0\), so that later steps do not undo earlier ones: \[ \mathbf s_{k+1} = -\mathbf g_{k+1} + \beta_{k+1}\mathbf s_k, \qquad \beta_{k+1} = \frac{\mathbf g_{k+1}^T\mathbf g_{k+1}}{\mathbf g_k^T\mathbf g_k} \ \ \text{(Fletcher–Reeves)}, \] with an exact line search along each \(\mathbf s_k\).

  • For a quadratic in \(n\) variables it reaches the exact minimum in at most \(n\) steps (in exact arithmetic).
  • It stores only a few vectors, no matrix: ideal for very large \(n\), and the basis of the iterative solver for large SPD systems \(A\mathbf x = \mathbf b\) (minimizing \(\tfrac12\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x - \mathbf b^T\mathbf x\)).
  • On non-quadratic \(f\) it is restarted every \(n\) steps or uses the Polak–Ribière \(\beta\).

Example

Example 4. Conjugate gradients for the quadratic of Example 1 from \((-2, 1)\).

Answer

The first step is the steepest descent step, to \((-1.809, 0.236)\). Then \(\beta_1 = \|\mathbf g_1\|^2/\|\mathbf g_0\|^2 = 10.52/68 = 0.155\), and the step along \(-\mathbf g_1 + \beta_1\mathbf s_0\) lands at \((0, 0)\) up to rounding: two steps for \(n = 2\). Steepest descent needs about 30 steps to reduce \(f\) by a factor of \(10^{10}\).
