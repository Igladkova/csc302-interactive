1. Tangent planes and linear approximation (Stewart §14.4)

If \(f\) has continuous partial derivatives, the tangent plane to \(z = f(x, y)\) at \((a, b, f(a, b))\) is (Stewart, Equation 14.4.2) \[ z = f(a, b) + f_x(a, b)(x - a) + f_y(a, b)(y - b). \] The right side, \(L(x, y)\), is the linearization of \(f\) at \((a, b)\), and \(f(x, y) \approx L(x, y)\) near \((a, b)\) is the linear approximation. It is the degree-1 Taylor polynomial in two variables.

  • Differentiability (Stewart, Definition 14.4.7) means \(\Delta z = f_x\,\Delta x + f_y\,\Delta y + \varepsilon_1 \Delta x + \varepsilon_2 \Delta y\) with \(\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2 \to 0\): the error of \(L\) is small compared with the step. If \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) exist near \((a, b)\) and are continuous at \((a, b)\), then \(f\) is differentiable there (Theorem 14.4.8). Merely having partial derivatives is not enough.
  • With continuous second partials, the error of \(L\) is of the size of the step squared.
  • Differentials: \(dz = f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy\) estimates the change \(\Delta z\) caused by small changes \(dx, dy\). With measurement errors in \(x\) and \(y\), \(|dz|\) estimates the largest error in \(z\).

Examples

Example 1. Find the tangent plane to \(z = x^2 + xy - y^2\) at \((1, 2)\) and use it to approximate \(f(1.1, 1.9)\).

Answer

\(f(1, 2) = 1 + 2 - 4 = -1\), \(f_x = 2x + y = 4\), \(f_y = x - 2y = -3\). Tangent plane: \(z = -1 + 4(x - 1) - 3(y - 2)\). \(L(1.1, 1.9) = -1 + 0.4 + 0.3 = -0.3\); the exact value is \(-0.31\).

Example 2. A cone has radius \(r = 3\) cm and height \(h = 4\) cm, each measured with error at most 0.1 cm. Estimate the largest error in its volume \(V = \frac13 \pi r^2 h\).

Answer

\(dV = \frac23 \pi r h\,dr + \frac13 \pi r^2\,dh = 8\pi(0.1) + 3\pi(0.1) = 1.1\pi \approx 3.46\ \text{cm}^3\), about 9.2% of \(V = 12\pi \approx 37.7\ \text{cm}^3\). (With both errors at +0.1 the actual change is 3.56 cm³.)

2. The chain rule (Stewart §14.5)

  • Case 1 (Stewart, 14.5.1): if \(z = f(x, y)\) with \(x = x(t)\), \(y = y(t)\), then \(\dfrac{dz}{dt} = \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}\dfrac{dx}{dt} + \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}\dfrac{dy}{dt}\).
  • Case 2 (14.5.2): if \(x = x(s, t)\), \(y = y(s, t)\), then \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial s} = z_x x_s + z_y y_s\) and \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial t} = z_x x_t + z_y y_t\).
  • General version (14.5.3): one term for each path in the tree diagram from \(z\) to the variable.
  • Matrix form: the row of partials of \(z\) with respect to \((s, t)\) is the row of partials of \(z\) with respect to \((x, y)\) times the Jacobian matrix of \((x, y)\) with respect to \((s, t)\): \[ \begin{bmatrix} z_s & z_t \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} z_x & z_y \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_s & x_t \\ y_s & y_t \end{bmatrix}. \] Composition of functions becomes multiplication of Jacobians, the form used for vector functions all semester.
  • Implicit differentiation: if \(F(x, y) = 0\) defines \(y\) as a function of \(x\), then \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{F_x}{F_y}\) (when \(F_y \ne 0\)). Similarly \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -F_x/F_z\) for \(F(x, y, z) = 0\).

Examples

Example 3. \(z = x^2 y\) with \(x = e^t\), \(y = \sin t\). Find \(dz/dt\) at \(t = 0\).

Answer

At \(t = 0\): \(x = 1\), \(y = 0\), \(dx/dt = e^0 = 1\), \(dy/dt = \cos 0 = 1\). \(dz/dt = 2xy\cdot 1 + x^2 \cdot 1 = 0 + 1 = 1\).

Example 4. \(z = e^x \cos y\) with \(x = st\), \(y = s - t\). Find \(z_s\) and \(z_t\) at \((s, t) = (1, 0)\) in matrix form.

Answer

At \((1, 0)\): \(x = 0\), \(y = 1\). \(z_x = e^x\cos y = \cos 1\), \(z_y = -e^x \sin y = -\sin 1\); Jacobian \(\begin{bmatrix} t & s \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\). \[ \begin{bmatrix} z_s & z_t \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos 1 & -\sin 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -\sin 1 & \cos 1 + \sin 1 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} -0.8415 & 1.3818 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Example 5. The curve \(x^2 + xy + y^3 = 3\) passes through \((1, 1)\). Find the slope \(dy/dx\) there.

Answer

\(F = x^2 + xy + y^3 - 3\), \(F_x = 2x + y = 3\), \(F_y = x + 3y^2 = 4\): \(dy/dx = -3/4\).

3. Directional derivatives and the gradient (Stewart §14.6)

The directional derivative of \(f\) at \((x_0, y_0)\) in the direction of a unit vector \(\mathbf u = \langle a, b\rangle\) is the rate of change of \(f\) along \(\mathbf u\) (Stewart, Definition 14.6.2). For differentiable \(f\) (Theorem 14.6.3, Definition 14.6.8): \[ D_{\mathbf u} f = f_x a + f_y b = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf u, \qquad \nabla f = \langle f_x, f_y\rangle . \]

  • Writing \(\nabla f \cdot \mathbf u = |\nabla f| \cos\theta\): the maximum of \(D_{\mathbf u} f\) is \(|\nabla f|\), in the direction of \(\nabla f\) (Theorem 14.6.15). The minimum is \(-|\nabla f|\), in the direction of \(-\nabla f\). The rate is 0 in the directions perpendicular to \(\nabla f\).
  • The gradient is perpendicular to the level curves of \(f\) (in three variables, to the level surfaces).
  • So the tangent plane to a level surface \(F(x, y, z) = k\) at \(P\) has normal vector \(\nabla F(P)\): \(F_x(P)(x - x_0) + F_y(P)(y - y_0) + F_z(P)(z - z_0) = 0\).
  • Always normalize the direction first: \(\mathbf u = \mathbf v/|\mathbf v|\).

Examples

Example 6. For \(f(x, y) = x e^y\) at \((2, 0)\): find \(\nabla f\), the rate of change toward the point \((5, 4)\), the maximum rate of change, and a direction in which \(f\) does not change.

Answer

\(\nabla f = \langle e^y, x e^y\rangle = \langle 1, 2\rangle\). Toward \((5, 4)\): \(\mathbf v = \langle 3, 4\rangle\), \(\mathbf u = \langle 0.6, 0.8\rangle\), \(D_{\mathbf u} f = 0.6 + 1.6 = 2.2\). Maximum rate \(|\nabla f| = \sqrt5 \approx 2.236\), in the direction \(\langle 1, 2\rangle/\sqrt5\). No change along \(\pm\langle 2, -1\rangle/\sqrt5\), the direction of the level curve.

Example 7. Find the tangent plane to the ellipsoid \(x^2 + 2y^2 + 3z^2 = 6\) at \((1, 1, 1)\).

Answer

\(\nabla F = \langle 2x, 4y, 6z\rangle = \langle 2, 4, 6\rangle\) at \((1, 1, 1)\): \(2(x - 1) + 4(y - 1) + 6(z - 1) = 0\), that is, \(x + 2y + 3z = 6\).

4. Why CSc 302 needs this

  • Linearization is the idea behind Newton's method for systems: replace each equation by its tangent plane and solve the linear system \(J\,\mathbf s = -\mathbf F\).
  • Differentials are error propagation: the same computation gives the condition number of an arithmetic operation.
  • The Jacobian and the matrix chain rule appear in Newton's and Broyden's methods, nonlinear least squares and change of variables in double integrals.
  • The gradient gives the steepest-descent direction, and \(\nabla f = 0\) is the first-order optimality condition. Parallel gradients, \(\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g\), are the Lagrange condition.

Review questions

  1. Write the tangent plane to \(z = f(x, y)\) at \((a, b)\). How is it related to the Taylor polynomial \(T_1\) in one variable?
  2. What does differentiability add beyond the existence of \(f_x\) and \(f_y\)? Which theorem gives a simple sufficient condition?
  3. How fast does the error of the linear approximation shrink as \((x, y) \to (a, b)\)?
  4. Use differentials to estimate the relative error in \(P = xy/z\) when \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) each have relative error at most 1%.
  5. Draw the tree diagram for \(w = f(x, y, z)\) with \(x, y, z\) functions of \(s, t\). How many terms has \(\partial w/\partial s\)?
  6. Write the chain rule as a product of Jacobian matrices. What are the sizes of the matrices for \(w = f(x, y, z)\), \((x, y, z) = \mathbf g(s, t)\)?
  7. Why must \(\mathbf u\) be a unit vector in \(D_{\mathbf u} f\)? In which directions is \(D_{\mathbf u} f\) largest, smallest and zero?
  8. Why is \(\nabla f\) perpendicular to the level curves? How do you use this to find a tangent plane to a level surface?
