Review: functions of several variables and partial derivatives

Learning objectives

  • Find and describe the domain of a function of two variables.
  • Read a function from its graph and from its level curves (contour map).
  • Decide whether a limit at a point exists: two different paths to show it does not, a bound (squeeze) to show it does.
  • Compute first and second partial derivatives, interpret them as slopes, and check Clairaut's Theorem.
  • Estimate a partial derivative from function values with a difference quotient.

1. Functions of two variables (Stewart §14.1)

A function of two variables assigns to each point \((x, y)\) of a set \(D \subset \mathbb{R}^2\), its domain, one real number \(f(x, y)\). Unless the domain is stated, it is the set of all points where the formula makes sense: no division by zero, no even root of a negative number, no logarithm of a non-positive number.

  • The graph of \(f\) is the surface \(z = f(x, y)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).
  • The level curves are the curves \(f(x, y) = k\) in the \(xy\)-plane: the horizontal slice of the surface at height \(k\), dropped onto the plane. A contour map draws them for equally spaced \(k\). Close contours mean a steep surface; far apart, a flat one.
  • For three variables, \(f(x, y, z) = k\) defines level surfaces.

Examples

Example 1. Find and sketch the domain of \(f(x, y) = \dfrac{\ln(9 - x^2 - y^2)}{\sqrt{x - y}}\).

Answer

The logarithm needs \(9 - x^2 - y^2 > 0\), the inside of the circle of radius 3 (circle excluded). The square root in the denominator needs \(x - y > 0\), the half-plane below the line \(y = x\) (line excluded). The domain is the intersection: the open half-disk \(\{x^2 + y^2 < 9,\ y < x\}\).

Example 2. Describe the level curves of \(f(x, y) = \dfrac{y - 1}{x^2}\).

Answer

\(f(x, y) = k\) means \(y = 1 + kx^2\) with \(x \ne 0\): parabolas with vertex \((0, 1)\), opening up for \(k > 0\), down for \(k < 0\), and the line \(y = 1\) for \(k = 0\), each with the point at \(x = 0\) removed. All of them pass arbitrarily close to \((0, 1)\), so \(f\) takes every value near that point.

2. Limits and continuity (Stewart §14.2)

\(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)} f(x, y) = L\) means that \(f(x, y)\) is as close to \(L\) as we like for all points of the domain close enough to \((a, b)\), whatever the direction of approach (Stewart, Definition 14.2.1). This gives two tools.

  • No limit: if \(f \to L_1\) along one path and \(f \to L_2 \ne L_1\) along another, the limit does not exist. Agreement along every straight line is not enough; a curved path can still disagree.
  • A limit exists: bound \(|f(x, y) - L|\) by something that tends to 0 (the Squeeze Theorem).

\(f\) is continuous at \((a, b)\) if \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)} f(x, y) = f(a, b)\) (Stewart, Definition 14.2.6). Polynomials, and rational functions on their domains, are continuous, and so are sums, products and compositions of continuous functions.

Examples

Example 3. Does \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} \frac{x^2 - y^2}{x^2 + y^2}\) exist?

Answer

No. Along the \(x\)-axis (\(y = 0\)) the function is \(x^2/x^2 = 1\); along the \(y\)-axis (\(x = 0\)) it is \(-y^2/y^2 = -1\). Two paths, two different limits.

Example 4. Show that \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} \frac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2} = 0\).

Answer

Since \(x^2 \le x^2 + y^2\), we have \(\left|\dfrac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2}\right| \le |y|\), and \(|y| \to 0\). By the Squeeze Theorem the limit is 0.

3. Partial derivatives (Stewart §14.3)

Hold one variable fixed and differentiate with respect to the other (Stewart, Definition 14.3.4): \[ f_x(a, b) = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(a + h, b) - f(a, b)}{h}, \qquad f_y(a, b) = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(a, b + h) - f(a, b)}{h}. \] Other notations: \(f_x = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x} = D_1 f\).

  • Geometry: \(f_x(a, b)\) is the slope of the curve cut from the surface by the plane \(y = b\) (the trace \(x \mapsto f(x, b)\)); \(f_y(a, b)\) is the slope of the trace \(y \mapsto f(a, y)\).
  • Rates: \(f_x\) is the rate of change of \(f\) when only \(x\) changes.
  • Second partials: \(f_{xx}\), \(f_{yy}\), and the mixed ones \(f_{xy} = (f_x)_y\) and \(f_{yx} = (f_y)_x\).
  • Clairaut's Theorem: if \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) are continuous on a disk around \((a, b)\), then \(f_{xy}(a, b) = f_{yx}(a, b)\).
  • Partial differential equations are written with partials. An example is Laplace's equation \(u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0\), satisfied by steady-state temperatures.

Examples

Example 5. For \(f(x, y) = x^3 - 2xy^2 + y\), find \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) and evaluate them at \((1, 2)\). What do the signs say?

Answer

\(f_x = 3x^2 - 2y^2\) and \(f_y = -4xy + 1\), so \(f_x(1, 2) = 3 - 8 = -5\) and \(f_y(1, 2) = -8 + 1 = -7\). Both are negative: moving from \((1, 2)\) in the positive \(x\)-direction or in the positive \(y\)-direction, \(f\) decreases, faster in the \(y\)-direction.

Example 6. Verify Clairaut's Theorem for \(g(x, y) = \sin(xy) + x^2 y\).

Answer

\(g_x = y\cos(xy) + 2xy\), so \(g_{xy} = \cos(xy) - xy\sin(xy) + 2x\). \(g_y = x\cos(xy) + x^2\), so \(g_{yx} = \cos(xy) - xy\sin(xy) + 2x\). They agree, as they must: both are continuous everywhere.

Example 7. Estimate \(f_x(1, 2)\) for the function of Example 5 with the forward difference \(\bigl(f(1 + h, 2) - f(1, 2)\bigr)/h\), \(h = 0.01\). Compare with the central difference \(\bigl(f(1 + h, 2) - f(1 - h, 2)\bigr)/(2h)\).

Answer

Forward: \(-4.9699\), error \(0.0301 \approx \tfrac{h}{2} f_{xx}(1, 2) = 0.005 \cdot 6\) (Taylor: the error is \(O(h)\)). Central: \(-4.9999\), error \(10^{-4}\) (\(O(h^2)\)). These are the formulas behind finite-difference partial derivatives in meeting 5 and Lab 3.

4. Why CSc 302 needs this

  • Contour maps are how we look at objective functions in optimization (meetings 20–23) and at the two equations of a nonlinear system (meeting 19).
  • Partial derivatives build the gradient, the Jacobian and the Hessian. These drive the tangent plane (meeting 6), Newton's method for systems (meeting 19) and the second-derivative test (meeting 20).
  • Difference quotients for partials are numerical approximations with a truncation–rounding trade-off, just as in one variable (meetings 1–3).
  • Limits along paths explain why having partial derivatives is not enough for a good linear approximation (meeting 6).

Review questions

  1. What restrictions decide the natural domain of a formula in \(x\) and \(y\)? Sketch the domain of \(\sqrt{y - x^2}\).
  2. How do you get a level curve from the graph of \(f\)? What do closely spaced contours tell you?
  3. Describe the level curves of \(x^2 + y^2\), \(x^2 - y^2\) and \(y - x\).
  4. Why does agreement of the limit along all straight lines through a point not prove that the limit exists?
  5. How do you show a limit exists using a bound? Try \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} \dfrac{xy^2}{x^2 + y^2}\).
  6. What is the geometric meaning of \(f_y(a, b)\)?
  7. State Clairaut's Theorem. Verify that \(u = \ln(x^2 + y^2)\) satisfies Laplace's equation.
  8. Which difference quotient for \(f_x\) has error \(O(h)\), and which \(O(h^2)\)? Why does the error grow again when \(h\) is very small?
