Meeting 11 summary: linear least squares — normal equations, projection, conditioning

Unit summary · Heath §3.1–3.3 · CSc 302 meeting 11

Learning objectives

  • Set up a data-fitting problem as an overdetermined linear least squares problem \(A\mathbf x \cong \mathbf b\), including models that are nonlinear in \(t\) but linear in the parameters.
  • Derive and solve the normal equations \(A^TA\mathbf x = A^T\mathbf b\), and say when the solution is unique.
  • Interpret the solution geometrically: \(A\mathbf x\) is the orthogonal projection of \(\mathbf b\) onto the column space of \(A\), and the residual is orthogonal to it.
  • Define \(\operatorname{cond}(A)\) for a rectangular matrix and explain how it, and the angle between \(\mathbf b\) and \(A\mathbf x\), govern sensitivity.
  • Explain why forming \(A^TA\) can lose twice as many digits as necessary.

This page summarizes Heath §3.1–3.3, CSc 302 meeting 11. It uses the linear-systems ideas of meetings 8–10. The QR factorization that solves least squares stably follows in meeting 12. Lab 7 practises both.

1. The least squares problem (Heath §3.1)

With more equations than unknowns (\(m > n\)), \(A\mathbf x = \mathbf b\) usually has no exact solution. Instead we minimize the 2-norm of the residual: \[ \min_{\mathbf x} \|\mathbf r\|_2^2 = \min_{\mathbf x} \|\mathbf b - A\mathbf x\|_2^2, \qquad A \in \mathbb R^{m\times n}. \] In data fitting with a model \(y \approx x_1\phi_1(t) + \cdots + x_n\phi_n(t)\), row \(i\) of \(A\) is \((\phi_1(t_i), \ldots, \phi_n(t_i))\) and \(b_i = y_i\). The problem is linear least squares as long as the model is linear in the unknown coefficients \(x_j\). The basis functions \(\phi_j\) can be anything: \(1, t, t^2\), \(\cos t\), \(e^{t}\). A model like \(y = x_1e^{x_2t}\) is nonlinear in \(x_2\): nonlinear least squares (Heath §6.6).

2. Existence, uniqueness, normal equations (Heath §3.2)

A minimizer always exists. It is unique exactly when the columns of \(A\) are linearly independent, \(\operatorname{rank}(A) = n\). Setting the gradient of \(\phi(\mathbf x) = \|\mathbf b - A\mathbf x\|_2^2\) to zero (meeting 20) gives the normal equations \[ A^TA\,\mathbf x = A^T\mathbf b \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad A^T\mathbf r = \mathbf 0 . \] If \(\operatorname{rank}(A) = n\), \(A^TA\) is symmetric positive definite, so Cholesky solves the normal equations (meeting 10). The second form says the residual is orthogonal to every column of \(A\). So \(\mathbf y = A\mathbf x\) is the orthogonal projection of \(\mathbf b\) onto \(\operatorname{span}(A)\): \[ \mathbf y = P\mathbf b, \qquad P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T, \qquad P^2 = P = P^T, \qquad \mathbf x = A^+\mathbf b, \quad A^+ = (A^TA)^{-1}A^T . \] \(A^+\) is the pseudo-inverse. If \(\operatorname{rank}(A) < n\), there are infinitely many minimizers, and \(A^+\mathbf b\) (computed by the SVD, meeting 14) is the one of smallest norm.

Examples

Example 1. Fit \(y = x_1 + x_2t^2\) to the data \((t, y) = (-1, 1), (0, 0), (1, 2), (2, 5)\).

Answer

Rows of \(A\) are \((1, t_i^2)\): \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}^T\). \(A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 6 & 18 \end{bmatrix}\), \(A^T\mathbf y = (8, 23)\). With \(\det = 36\): \(x_1 = (18\cdot8 - 6\cdot23)/36 = 1/6\), \(x_2 = (4\cdot23 - 6\cdot8)/36 = 11/9\). So \(y = 0.1667 + 1.2222\,t^2\). Residual \(\mathbf r = (-0.389, -0.167, 0.611, -0.056)\), \(\|\mathbf r\|_2 = 0.745\); check \(A^T\mathbf r = \mathbf 0\). The model is quadratic in \(t\), yet linear least squares applies.

Example 2. Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf b = (1, 2, 6)\). Solve the least squares problem, find the projection and the residual, and write the projector \(P\).

Answer

\(A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), \(A^T\mathbf b = (7, 8)\), so \(\mathbf x = (2, 3)\). Projection \(\mathbf y = A\mathbf x = (2, 3, 5)\); residual \(\mathbf r = (-1, -1, 1)\), with \(A^T\mathbf r = (0, 0)\) and \(\|\mathbf r\|_2^2 = 3\). \(P = \tfrac13\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\); check \(P\mathbf b = \mathbf y\) and \(P^2 = P\). Here \(A^+ = \tfrac13\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\). The figure shows this projection.

3. Sensitivity and conditioning (Heath §3.3)

For a rectangular \(A\) of full column rank, \(\operatorname{cond}(A) = \|A\|_2\,\|A^+\|_2 = \sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}\). How much the solution moves also depends on the angle \(\theta\) between \(\mathbf b\) and its projection \(A\mathbf x\), with \(\cos\theta = \|A\mathbf x\|_2/\|\mathbf b\|_2\): \[ \frac{\|\Delta\mathbf x\|_2}{\|\mathbf x\|_2} \le \operatorname{cond}(A)\,\frac{1}{\cos\theta}\,\frac{\|\Delta\mathbf b\|_2}{\|\mathbf b\|_2}, \qquad \frac{\|\Delta\mathbf x\|_2}{\|\mathbf x\|_2} \lesssim \bigl([\operatorname{cond}(A)]^2\tan\theta + \operatorname{cond}(A)\bigr)\frac{\|E\|_2}{\|A\|_2}. \] If the fit is good (\(\theta\) small), the problem behaves like a square system with condition \(\operatorname{cond}(A)\). If the fit is poor (\(\theta\) near \(90^\circ\)), the sensitivity grows, up to \(\operatorname{cond}(A)^2\).

The normal equations square the condition number: \(\operatorname{cond}(A^TA) = \operatorname{cond}(A)^2\). Forming \(A^TA\) in floating point can lose information. For example, \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ \epsilon & 0 \\ 0 & \epsilon \end{bmatrix}\) with \(\epsilon < \sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{mach}}}\) gives a computed \(A^TA\) that is exactly singular. Solving the normal equations loses about \(2\log_{10}\operatorname{cond}(A)\) digits, even when the problem itself (small \(\theta\)) only warrants \(\log_{10}\operatorname{cond}(A)\). QR (meeting 12) avoids forming \(A^TA\).

Example

Example 3. For Example 2, find \(\operatorname{cond}(A)\) and the angle \(\theta\). Then fit polynomials of degree \(d\) to 40 equally spaced points on \([0, 1]\), with data chosen so that all coefficients are exactly 1. Compare the coefficient errors of the normal equations and of QR.

Answer

Example 2: the singular values are \(\sqrt3\) and 1 (square roots of the eigenvalues 3 and 1 of \(A^TA\)), so \(\operatorname{cond}(A) = 1.73\). \(\cos\theta = \sqrt{38}/\sqrt{41} = 0.963\), \(\theta = 15.7^\circ\): well conditioned. Polynomial fits (monomial basis):

6. Try it, and review questions

Interactive: Data fitting. Lab 7 fits data by normal equations and QR, and builds an AR time-series model.

  1. Which models are linear least squares problems in the unknowns \(a, b\): \(y = a + b\ln t\), \(y = a\,t^b\), \(y = a\sin t + b\,t\)? How can you turn \(y = a\,t^b\) into one?
  2. Derive the normal equations by setting the gradient of \(\|\mathbf b - A\mathbf x\|_2^2\) to zero.
  3. Why is the least squares solution unique exactly when \(A\) has full column rank?
  4. Show that \(P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T\) satisfies \(P^2 = P\). What does \(I - P\) project onto?
  5. If \(\mathbf b\) lies in \(\operatorname{span}(A)\), what are \(\mathbf r\) and \(\theta\)?
  6. \(\operatorname{cond}(A) = 10^6\). About how many correct digits do the normal equations give in double precision? QR?
  7. Give a \(3\times2\) matrix for which the computed \(A^TA\) is singular although \(A\) has full rank.
  8. Why does a poor fit (large \(\theta\)) make the solution more sensitive to changes in \(A\)?
