1. Symmetric positive definite systems and Cholesky (Heath §2.5.1)

\(A\) is symmetric positive definite if \(A = A^T\) and \(\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x > 0\) for every \(\mathbf x \ne \mathbf 0\); equivalently, \(A\) is symmetric with all eigenvalues positive. SPD matrices arise as \(B^TB\) with \(B\) of full column rank (least squares, meeting 11), as Hessians at a minimum (meeting 20), and as stiffness matrices in physics. Every SPD matrix factors as \[ A = LL^T, \qquad \ell_{jj} = \Bigl(a_{jj} - \sum_{k<j}\ell_{jk}^2\Bigr)^{1/2}, \qquad \ell_{ij} = \Bigl(a_{ij} - \sum_{k<j}\ell_{ik}\ell_{jk}\Bigr)\Big/\ell_{jj} \ \ (i > j), \] with \(L\) lower triangular with positive diagonal.

  • No pivoting needed: \(\sum_k \ell_{ik}^2 = a_{ii}\), so the entries of \(L\) cannot grow; the algorithm is stable.
  • Half the cost and storage of LU: about \(n^3/6\) multiplications, plus \(n\) square roots; only the lower triangle is stored.
  • Test for SPD: a symmetric matrix is positive definite exactly when Cholesky runs to completion (every number under a square root is positive). numpy.linalg.cholesky raises LinAlgError otherwise.
  • Symmetric indefinite matrices use \(A = LDL^T\) with symmetric pivoting (Heath §2.5.2).

Examples

Example 1. Compute the Cholesky factor of \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 9 & -3 & 3 \\ -3 & 5 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 & 11 \end{bmatrix}\) and use it to solve \(A\mathbf x = (9, 3, 15)\).

Answer

Column 1: \(\ell_{11} = 3\), \(\ell_{21} = -3/3 = -1\), \(\ell_{31} = 3/3 = 1\). Column 2: \(\ell_{22} = \sqrt{5 - 1} = 2\), \(\ell_{32} = (1 - (1)(-1))/2 = 1\). Column 3: \(\ell_{33} = \sqrt{11 - 1 - 1} = 3\). \[ L = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}. \] \(L\mathbf y = \mathbf b\) gives \(\mathbf y = (3, 3, 3)\); \(L^T\mathbf x = \mathbf y\) gives \(\mathbf x = (1, 1, 1)\). All square roots were of positive numbers, so \(A\) is SPD (its eigenvalues are 2.71, 9 and 13.29).

Example 2. Is \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\) positive definite?

Answer

No. \(\ell_{11} = 1\), \(\ell_{21} = 2\), and then \(\ell_{22}^2 = 3 - 4 = -1 < 0\): Cholesky fails. Indeed \(\mathbf x = (2, -1)\) gives \(\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x = 4 - 8 + 3 = -1\), and the eigenvalues are \(2 \pm \sqrt5 = 4.24, -0.24\). Positive diagonal entries are not enough.

2. Banded and tridiagonal systems (Heath §2.5.3)

A matrix has bandwidth \(\beta\) if \(a_{ij} = 0\) for \(|i - j| > \beta\); a tridiagonal matrix has \(\beta = 1\). Such matrices come from splines (meeting 25), finite differences for differential equations, and chains of springs or circuits. Without pivoting, elimination creates no new nonzeros outside the band, so \(L\) and \(U\) are banded too (figure). The cost drops from \(n^3/3\) to \(O(n\beta^2)\), and storage from \(n^2\) to \(O(n\beta)\). For a tridiagonal matrix (Thomas algorithm), one forward sweep and one back sweep take about \(8n\) operations. Pivoting is unnecessary if the matrix is SPD or diagonally dominant; otherwise partial pivoting widens the band of \(U\) only slightly.

Examples

Example 3. Solve the tridiagonal system with 4 on the diagonal, 1 on the sub- and superdiagonals, and \(\mathbf b = (6, 12, 18, 19)\) (\(n = 4\)).

Answer

Forward sweep, \(d_i \leftarrow d_i - m\,c_{i-1}\) and \(b_i \leftarrow b_i - m\,b_{i-1}\) with \(m = 1/d_{i-1}\): \(m = 0.25\): \(d_2 = 3.75\), \(b_2 = 10.5\); \(m = 0.2667\): \(d_3 = 3.7333\), \(b_3 = 15.2\); \(m = 0.2679\): \(d_4 = 3.7321\), \(b_4 = 14.929\). Back sweep: \(x_4 = 14.929/3.7321 = 4\), \(x_3 = (15.2 - 4)/3.7333 = 3\), \(x_2 = (10.5 - 3)/3.75 = 2\), \(x_1 = (6 - 2)/4 = 1\). The matrix is diagonally dominant, so no pivoting was needed and the multipliers stayed small.

Example 4. A tridiagonal system has \(n = 10^5\). Compare dense LU with the tridiagonal solver in operations and storage (8 bytes per number).

Answer

Dense: \(n^3/3 \approx 3.3\times10^{14}\) multiplications and \(n^2 = 10^{10}\) numbers, 80 GB. Tridiagonal: about \(8n = 8\times10^5\) operations and \(3n\) numbers, 2.4 MB. The structure, not a faster computer, makes the problem solvable.

3. Software (Heath §2.7)

LAPACK provides the factorizations, built on the BLAS (Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms). Level-3 BLAS (matrix–matrix operations) do most of the work and run near the machine's peak speed. NumPy and SciPy call LAPACK:
