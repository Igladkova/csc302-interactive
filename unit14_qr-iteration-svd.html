1. QR iteration (Heath §4.5.6)

Start with \(A_0 = A\) and repeat \[ A_k = Q_kR_k \ \ (\text{QR factorization}), \qquad A_{k+1} = R_kQ_k = Q_k^TA_kQ_k . \] Each \(A_{k+1}\) is orthogonally similar to \(A_k\), so the eigenvalues never change. Under mild conditions (distinct \(|\lambda_i|\)), \(A_k\) converges to upper triangular form, diagonal if \(A\) is symmetric, with the eigenvalues on the diagonal in decreasing magnitude. The subdiagonal entry \((i+1, i)\) decays like \(|\lambda_{i+1}/\lambda_i|^k\). In effect QR iteration is simultaneous power iteration on all columns, with re-orthogonalization at each step. Practical algorithms add:

  • Shifts: factor \(A_k - \sigma_kI\), then add \(\sigma_kI\) back. With \(\sigma_k = (A_k)_{nn}\) (Rayleigh quotient shift), the last row converges quadratically (cubically if symmetric), as in Rayleigh quotient iteration.
  • Deflation: once a subdiagonal entry is negligible, split off the converged eigenvalue and continue with a smaller matrix.
  • Hessenberg reduction: first reduce \(A\) by Householder similarity transformations to upper Hessenberg form (tridiagonal if symmetric), at a one-time cost of \(O(n^3)\). Each QR step then costs \(O(n^2)\) (\(O(n)\) if tridiagonal) instead of \(O(n^3)\). Computing all eigenvalues then takes about \(10n^3\) operations for a general matrix and \(\tfrac43n^3\) for a symmetric one (more if eigenvectors are also wanted).

Examples

Example 1. Do unshifted QR iteration on \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) (eigenvalues \(2 \pm \sqrt2 = 3.4142, 0.5858\)).

Answer

\(Q_0 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\), \(R_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 3.1623 & 1.2649 \\ 0 & 0.6325 \end{bmatrix}\), \(A_1 = R_0Q_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 3.4 & 0.2 \\ 0.2 & 0.6 \end{bmatrix}\). Continuing: \(A_2\) has off-diagonal \(0.0345\) and diagonal \((3.4138, 0.5862)\); \(A_3\) has \(0.0059\); \(A_4\) has \(0.0010\). Each step multiplies the off-diagonal by about \(\lambda_2/\lambda_1 = 0.172\). The trace stays 4 throughout.

Example 2. The same matrix with the shift \(\sigma_k = (A_k)_{22}\).

Answer

Off-diagonal entries: \(0.2\) (shift 1), then \(1.0\times10^{-3}\) (shift 0.6), then \(1.3\times10^{-10}\) (shift 0.585787). Two steps after the first reach the eigenvalue \(0.585786\) to 16 digits: cubic convergence for a symmetric matrix, versus a factor of 0.17 per step without shifts.

2. The singular value decomposition (Heath §3.6, §4.7)

Every \(m\times n\) matrix factors as \[ A = U\Sigma V^T, \qquad U^TU = I, \ V^TV = I, \qquad \Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge 0) . \] Geometrically, \(A\) maps the unit sphere to an ellipsoid with semi-axes \(\sigma_i\mathbf u_i\); \(A\mathbf v_i = \sigma_i\mathbf u_i\) (figure). The \(\sigma_i^2\) are the eigenvalues of \(A^TA\), and the \(\mathbf v_i\) its eigenvectors. Uses:

  • \(\|A\|_2 = \sigma_1\); \(\operatorname{cond}_2(A) = \sigma_1/\sigma_{\min}\); the numerical rank is the number of \(\sigma_i\) above a tolerance;
  • pseudo-inverse \(A^+ = V\Sigma^+U^T\) (invert the nonzero \(\sigma_i\)): the minimum-norm least squares solution (meeting 11);
  • best rank-\(k\) approximation (Eckart–Young): \(A_k = \sum_{i\le k}\sigma_i\mathbf u_i\mathbf v_i^T\), with \(\|A - A_k\|_2 = \sigma_{k+1}\). This is the basis of image compression and PCA.

How it is computed: not from \(A^TA\), whose condition number is \(\operatorname{cond}(A)^2\), so the small \(\sigma_i\) would lose all accuracy. Instead, Householder transformations from both sides reduce \(A\) to bidiagonal form, and a QR-iteration variant (Golub–Kahan) or a divide-and-conquer method finishes the job (Heath §4.7). The cost is a few times that of QR.

Examples

Example 3. Find the singular values of \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), and \(\operatorname{cond}_2(B)\).

Answer

\(B^TB = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\), with eigenvalues \(3 \pm 2\sqrt2 = (\sqrt2 \pm 1)^2\). So \(\sigma_1 = 1 + \sqrt2 = 2.4142\) and \(\sigma_2 = \sqrt2 - 1 = 0.4142\), and \(\operatorname{cond}_2(B) = (1 + \sqrt2)^2 = 5.83\). Note \(\sigma_1\sigma_2 = 1 = |\det B|\), while both eigenvalues of \(B\) equal 1: singular values, not eigenvalues, measure stretching. \(\mathbf v_1 = (0.383, 0.924)\) is mapped to \(\sigma_1\mathbf u_1\) with \(\mathbf u_1 = (0.924, 0.383)\).

Example 4. \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 + 10^{-8} \end{bmatrix}\) has \(\sigma_2 = 5.0\times10^{-9}\). Compare numpy.linalg.svd with the square roots of the eigenvalues of \(A^TA\) computed in double precision.

Answer

Exact: \(\sigma_1 = 2.000000005\), \(\sigma_2 = 4.99999999\times10^{-9}\). svd returns \(\sigma_2 = 4.99999993\times10^{-9}\), correct to 7 digits; the small error comes from storing \(1 + 10^{-8}\) in binary. Via \(A^TA\): the small eigenvalue \(\sigma_2^2 = 2.5\times10^{-17}\) is below the rounding level \(\epsilon_{\text{mach}}\|A^TA\|_2 \approx 9\times10^{-16}\), and eigvalsh returns exactly 0, so \(\sigma_2\) is lost completely. In general the SVD algorithm finds each \(\sigma_i\) to within about \(\epsilon_{\text{mach}}\sigma_1\), while the \(A^TA\) route loses accuracy for every \(\sigma_i\) below \(\sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{mach}}}\,\sigma_1 \approx 10^{-8}\sigma_1\).

  1. Show that \(A_{k+1} = R_kQ_k\) is similar to \(A_k\).
