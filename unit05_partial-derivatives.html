Learning objectives

  • Compute first and higher partial derivatives, and interpret \(f_x\), \(f_y\) as slopes of the traces of a surface.
  • State Clairaut's theorem, and use it to check mixed partials.
  • Approximate partial derivatives by forward, centered and mixed finite differences, and state their orders.
  • Predict how finite-difference errors change with \(h\), including the rounding floor, and count the function evaluations a finite-difference gradient needs.

1. Partial derivatives (Stewart §14.3)

Hold all variables but one fixed and differentiate: \[ f_x(a, b) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a + h, b) - f(a, b)}{h}, \qquad f_y(a, b) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a, b + h) - f(a, b)}{h} . \] Geometrically, \(f_x(a, b)\) is the slope of the curve cut from the surface by the plane \(y = b\) (a trace), and \(f_y(a, b)\) the slope of the trace in \(x = a\) (figure). All one-variable rules apply, with the other variables treated as constants.

Higher derivatives: \(f_{xx}\), \(f_{yy}\), and the mixed \(f_{xy} = (f_x)_y\), \(f_{yx}\). Clairaut's theorem: if \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) are continuous near \((a, b)\), they are equal there. The second derivatives form the Hessian matrix, which decides between minimum, maximum and saddle (meeting 20). Partial differential equations, such as Laplace's equation \(u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0\), are written with them.

Examples

Example 1. For \(f(x, y) = x^2\sin y + y\,e^x\), find \(f_x\), \(f_y\), \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\), and evaluate them at \((1, 0.5)\).

Answer

\(f_x = 2x\sin y + y\,e^x\), \(f_y = x^2\cos y + e^x\). \(f_{xy} = 2x\cos y + e^x = f_{yx}\), as Clairaut predicts. At \((1, 0.5)\): \(f_x = 2.31799\), \(f_y = 3.59586\), \(f_{xy} = 4.47345\).

Example 2. Show that \(u(x, y) = x^3 - 3xy^2\) satisfies Laplace's equation.

Answer

\(u_x = 3x^2 - 3y^2\), \(u_{xx} = 6x\); \(u_y = -6xy\), \(u_{yy} = -6x\). So \(u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0\): \(u\) is harmonic (it is the real part of \((x + iy)^3\)).

2. Finite-difference partial derivatives (Heath §8.6.1)

When \(f\) is given by a program or by data, approximate the limits by difference quotients with a small step \(h\). Taylor expansion in one variable at a time gives the errors: \[ f_x \approx \frac{f(x + h, y) - f(x, y)}{h} \ \ (O(h)), \qquad f_x \approx \frac{f(x + h, y) - f(x - h, y)}{2h} \ \ (O(h^2)), \] \[ f_{xy} \approx \frac{f(x + h, y + h) - f(x + h, y - h) - f(x - h, y + h) + f(x - h, y - h)}{4h^2} \ \ (O(h^2)) . \] Each value of \(f\) also carries a rounding error of about \(\epsilon_{\text{mach}}|f|\), which the division by \(h\) (or \(h^2\)) amplifies. The total error therefore first falls and then rises as \(h\) decreases. The best \(h\) is about \(\sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{mach}}} \approx 10^{-8}\) for the forward difference and \(\epsilon_{\text{mach}}^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5}\) for the centered one (meeting 28).

Cost: a forward-difference gradient of \(f:\mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R\) needs \(n + 1\) evaluations of \(f\), a centered one \(2n\). For large \(n\) this becomes expensive, one reason for automatic differentiation (meeting 28).

Examples

Example 3. For \(f\) of Example 1 at \((1, 0.5)\), approximate \(f_y\) by forward and centered differences, and \(f_{xy}\) by the mixed formula, with \(h = 0.1\) and \(h = 0.01\).

Answer

Forward \(f_y\): \(3.57045\) and \(3.59345\), errors \(-2.5\times10^{-2}\) and \(-2.4\times10^{-3}\) (factor 10: first order). Centered \(f_y\): \(3.59440\) and \(3.595850\), errors \(-1.5\times10^{-3}\) and \(-1.5\times10^{-5}\) (factor 100: second order). Mixed \(f_{xy}\): \(4.47506\) and \(4.473463\), errors \(1.6\times10^{-3}\) and \(1.6\times10^{-5}\) (second order).

Example 4. A simulation \(f(x_1, \ldots, x_{500})\) takes 2 seconds per evaluation. How long does a centered-difference gradient take? A forward-difference one?

Answer

Centered: \(2n = 1000\) evaluations, about 33 minutes. Forward: \(n + 1 = 501\), about 17 minutes, but with only about half as many correct digits. If the code can be differentiated automatically (reverse mode), the gradient costs a small multiple of one evaluation (meeting 28).

5. Try it, and review questions

Interactive: Partial derivatives and finite differences. Lab 3 computes finite-difference partials and plots their errors against \(h\).

  1. Interpret \(f_y(a, b)\) geometrically. Which curve on the surface has it as its slope?
  2. Find all second partial derivatives of \(f(x, y) = e^{xy}\cos x\), and check Clairaut's theorem.
  3. Is \(u = e^{x}\cos y\) harmonic? Is \(u = x^2 + y^2\)?
  4. Derive the error term of the centered difference for \(f_x\) from Taylor's theorem.
  5. A forward difference has error \(3\times10^{-3}\) at \(h = 0.01\). Predict the error at \(h = 0.001\) and at \(h = 10^{-12}\).
  6. Why does the mixed-difference formula divide by \(4h^2\)? What happens to its rounding error as \(h \to 0\)?
  7. How many function evaluations does a centered-difference Hessian of a function of \(n\) variables need, roughly?
