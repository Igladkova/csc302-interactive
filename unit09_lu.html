1. Triangular systems (Heath §2.4.1–2.4.2)

The strategy is to transform \(A\mathbf x = \mathbf b\) into a system with the same solution that is easy to solve. Triangular systems are easy: for upper triangular \(U\), back substitution gives \[ x_n = b_n/u_{nn}, \qquad x_i = \Bigl(b_i - \sum_{j > i} u_{ij}x_j\Bigr)\Big/u_{ii}, \quad i = n - 1, \ldots, 1, \] and lower triangular systems are solved by forward substitution in the same way, from the top. Each costs about \(n^2/2\) multiplications. A triangular matrix is nonsingular exactly when all its diagonal entries are nonzero.

Example

Example 1. Solve \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 3 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 13 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix}\).

Answer

\(x_3 = 8/4 = 2\); \(x_2 = (13 - 2\cdot2)/3 = 3\); \(x_1 = (3 - 3 + 2)/2 = 1\). So \(\mathbf x = (1, 3, 2)\).

2. Gaussian elimination is LU factorization (Heath §2.4.3–2.4.4)

To zero the entries below the pivot \(a_{kk}\), subtract multiples \(m_i = a_{ik}/a_{kk}\) of row \(k\) from rows \(i > k\). This is multiplication by an elementary elimination matrix \(M_k = I - \mathbf m_k\mathbf e_k^T\), whose inverse is \(L_k = I + \mathbf m_k\mathbf e_k^T\) (just flip the signs). After \(n - 1\) steps \(M_{n-1}\cdots M_1 A = U\), so \[ A = LU, \qquad L = L_1 L_2 \cdots L_{n-1} = \text{unit lower triangular, with the multipliers } m_{ik} \text{ below the diagonal}. \] Then \(A\mathbf x = \mathbf b\) is solved as \(L\mathbf y = \mathbf b\) (forward) and \(U\mathbf x = \mathbf y\) (back).

Example

Example 2. Factor \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) without pivoting, and solve \(A\mathbf x = (4, 5, 1)\).

Answer

Step 1: multipliers \(m_{21} = 2\), \(m_{31} = -1\) give rows \((0, -1, -2)\) and \((0, 3, 2)\). Step 2: \(m_{32} = 3/(-1) = -3\) gives row \((0, 0, -4)\). So \[ L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}. \] Forward: \(\mathbf y = (4, -3, -4)\). Back: \(x_3 = 1\), \(x_2 = (-3 + 2)/(-1) = 1\), \(x_1 = (4 - 1 - 1)/2 = 1\). So \(\mathbf x = (1, 1, 1)\).

3. Pivoting (Heath §2.4.5)

Elimination fails if a pivot is zero, and is inaccurate if a pivot is small: the multipliers are huge, and adding huge multiples of one row to another swamps the data in rounding. Partial pivoting swaps rows so that the pivot is the entry of largest magnitude in its column (on or below the diagonal). Then every \(|m_{ik}| \le 1\), and the result is \[ PA = LU, \qquad P = \text{permutation matrix recording the row swaps}. \] With partial pivoting, Gaussian elimination is stable in practice. Element growth can be as large as \(2^{n-1}\) in rare contrived cases, but practically never happens. Complete pivoting (rows and columns) is safer still but costs more searching, and is rarely used. No pivoting is needed for symmetric positive definite or diagonally dominant matrices (meeting 10).

Example

Example 3. Solve \(\begin{bmatrix} 0.0001 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\) in 3-digit decimal arithmetic (round every result to 3 significant digits), without and with pivoting. The exact solution is \((1.0001, 0.9999)\).

Answer

No pivoting: \(m = 10^4\); \(a_{22} = 1 - 10^4 \to -1.00\times10^4\), \(b_2 = 2 - 10^4 \to -1.00\times10^4\), so \(x_2 = 1.00\) and \(x_1 = (1 - 1)/0.0001 = 0\): completely wrong. The information in \(a_{22} = 1\) and \(b_2 = 2\) was rounded away. Partial pivoting (swap rows): \(m = 0.0001\); \(a_{22} = 1 - 0.0001 \to 1.00\), \(b_2 = 1 - 0.0002 \to 1.00\), so \(x_2 = 1.00\), \(x_1 = 2 - 1 = 1.00\): correct to 3 digits. The matrix is well conditioned (\(\operatorname{cond}_\infty \approx 4\)), so the failure is the algorithm's fault, not the problem's.

4. Cost; factor once, solve many (Heath §2.4.7–2.4.9)

  • LU factorization: about \(n^3/3\) multiplications and as many additions. Each solve with \(L\) and \(U\): about \(n^2\).
  • For \(k\) right-hand sides with the same \(A\), factor once and solve \(k\) times: \(n^3/3 + kn^2\), not \(k\cdot n^3/3\). In SciPy: lu, piv = scipy.linalg.lu_factor(A), then lu_solve((lu, piv), b) for each \(\mathbf b\).
  • Do not form \(A^{-1}\) to solve a system: computing it costs about \(n^3\) (three times LU), multiplying \(A^{-1}\mathbf b\) costs \(n^2\) anyway, and the result is less accurate. Read \(A^{-1}B\) as "solve \(AX = B\)".
  • Gauss–Jordan elimination (reduce to diagonal form) costs about \(n^3/2\) and is used mainly to compute inverses.
  • If \(A\) changes by a rank-one matrix, the Sherman–Morrison formula reuses the old factorization instead of refactoring (Heath §2.4.9).

Example

Example 4. \(n = 1000\) and 100 right-hand sides. Compare the multiplication counts for (a) refactoring each time, (b) factor once, solve 100 times, (c) forming \(A^{-1}\) and multiplying.

Answer

(a) \(100 \times n^3/3 \approx 3.3\times10^{10}\). (b) \(n^3/3 + 100n^2 \approx 3.3\times10^{8} + 10^{8} = 4.3\times10^{8}\), about 77 times less. (c) \(n^3 + 100n^2 \approx 1.1\times10^{9}\), more than (b), and less accurate.

Interactive: Gaussian elimination (LU) and Gauss–Jordan elimination. Lab 5 implements LU with and without pivoting and measures growth.

  1. Write the elementary elimination matrix that zeros the entries below \(a_{11}\) in a \(3\times3\) matrix. What is its inverse?
  2. Why does \(L\) contain the multipliers with their original signs?
  3. Factor \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 8 \end{bmatrix}\) as \(LU\) and solve with \(\mathbf b = (5, 19)\).
  4. Which matrix needs pivoting even though it is nonsingular: \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) or \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\)? What does \(P\) do?
  5. With partial pivoting, why is \(|m_{ik}| \le 1\)? Why does that help?
  6. Derive the \(n^3/3\) count for LU from \(\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(n - k)^2\).
