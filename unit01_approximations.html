1. Scientific computing and its strategy (Heath §1.1)

Scientific computing (numerical analysis) designs and analyses algorithms for problems of continuous mathematics: equations, integrals, derivatives, optimization, data fitting. Most such problems have no closed-form solution, or one too costly to evaluate, so we compute an approximate answer and must also judge how accurate it is. The general strategy is to replace a hard problem by an easier one with the same solution, or a nearby one:

  • infinite \(\to\) finite: a series by a partial sum, an integral by a weighted sum (meeting 26), a derivative by a difference quotient (meeting 5);
  • nonlinear \(\to\) linear: Newton's method replaces \(f\) by its tangent line or tangent plane (meetings 18–19);
  • complicated \(\to\) simple: a function by a polynomial (Taylor here; interpolation in meeting 24); a general matrix by a triangular one (meeting 9).

Taylor's theorem is the main tool for analysing the error of this replacement, and it runs through the whole course.

2. Sources of approximation (Heath §1.2.1)

Before the computationDuring the computation
Modeling: physics simplified (no air resistance, Earth as a sphere)Truncation (discretization): an infinite or continuous process cut off; a series truncated, \(h > 0\) instead of \(h \to 0\)
Measurement: data known to a few digitsRounding: real numbers stored and combined with finitely many digits (meeting 3)
Previous computations: inputs that are themselves approximate

The final accuracy depends on all of them. Effort spent shrinking one error far below the others is wasted: computing to 16 digits with data known to 3 does not give a 16-digit answer.

3. Taylor's theorem with remainder (Stewart §11.10–11.11)

If \(f\) has \(n + 1\) continuous derivatives near \(a\), then with \(h = x - a\) \[ f(x) = \underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}h^k}_{T_n(x)} + R_n(x), \qquad R_n(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n + 1)!}h^{n+1} \ \text{ for some } \xi \text{ between } a \text{ and } x . \] If \(|f^{(n+1)}| \le M\) between \(a\) and \(x\) (Taylor's inequality): \[ |f(x) - T_n(x)| \le \frac{M}{(n + 1)!}|h|^{n+1} = O(h^{n+1}) . \] Two uses: (1) for fixed \(x\), raise \(n\) until the bound meets the tolerance; (2) for fixed \(n\), the error shrinks like \(h^{n+1}\), so halving \(h\) divides it by about \(2^{n+1}\). Use (2) is how every method in the course gets its "order".

Examples

Example 1. Approximate \(\sin 0.4\) with \(T_3(x) = x - x^3/6\) about 0, and bound the error.

Answer

\(T_3(0.4) = 0.4 - 0.064/6 = 0.389333\). The \(x^4\) coefficient of sine is zero, so \(T_3 = T_4\) and we may use the remainder \(R_4\): \(|R_4| \le \frac{1}{5!}(0.4)^5 = 8.53\times10^{-5}\) (since \(|\sin^{(5)}| \le 1\)). Actual error: \(\sin 0.4 - T_3(0.4) = 0.389418 - 0.389333 = 8.50\times10^{-5}\), so the bound is nearly sharp.

Example 2. How many terms of \(e = \sum_{k\ge0} 1/k!\) guarantee an error below \(10^{-6}\)?

Answer

With \(a = 0\), \(x = 1\) and \(M = e^1 < 3\): \(|R_n| \le 3/(n + 1)!\). Then \(n = 8\) gives \(8.3\times10^{-6}\) (not enough) and \(n = 9\) gives \(8.3\times10^{-7}\). So \(T_9\), with 10 terms, suffices. Its actual error is \(3.0\times10^{-7}\).

Example 3. The error of \(T_2(h) = h - h^2/2\) for \(\ln(1 + h)\) is \(2.32\times10^{-3}\) at \(h = 0.2\). Predict it at \(h = 0.1\) and \(0.05\).

Answer

The error is \(O(h^3)\) (leading term \(h^3/3\)), so each halving divides it by about 8: predictions \(2.9\times10^{-4}\) and \(3.6\times10^{-5}\). Actual: \(3.10\times10^{-4}\) and \(4.02\times10^{-5}\), ratios 7.5 and 7.7, approaching 8 as \(h\) shrinks.

6. Try it, and review questions

Interactive: Infinite series and Rounding vs truncation error. Lab 2 plots Taylor polynomials and their errors.

  1. Give three examples of the "replace a hard problem by an easier one" strategy.
  2. List four sources of error in computing the range of a thrown ball from measured speed and angle.
  3. Write \(T_3\) for \(\cos x\) about \(a = \pi/3\) and bound its error on \([\pi/3, \pi/3 + 0.1]\).
  4. How many terms of the Maclaurin series of \(e^{-x}\) give \(e^{-0.5}\) to within \(10^{-8}\)?
  5. Why does the Maclaurin series of \(\ln(1 + x)\) fail to approximate \(\ln 3\)? How could you compute \(\ln 3\) instead?
  6. A method has error \(5\times10^{-4}\) at \(h = 0.1\) and \(6.3\times10^{-5}\) at \(h = 0.05\). What is its order?
  7. Why is it pointless to compute an answer to 16 digits when the data are known to 3?
