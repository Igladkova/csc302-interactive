Learning objectives

  • Write the tangent plane and the linearization of \(f(x, y)\), and use them to approximate values.
  • Use differentials to propagate measurement errors, and connect this to condition numbers.
  • Form the Jacobian matrix of a vector function \(\mathbf F:\mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^m\), and write the linear approximation \(\mathbf F(\mathbf x + \mathbf s) \approx \mathbf F(\mathbf x) + J(\mathbf x)\mathbf s\).
  • Write the second-order Taylor expansion with the gradient and Hessian, and predict the errors of the linear and quadratic models.

1. Tangent planes and linear approximation (Stewart §14.4)

If \(f\) is differentiable at \((a, b)\), the surface \(z = f(x, y)\) has the tangent plane \[ z = L(x, y) = f(a, b) + f_x(a, b)(x - a) + f_y(a, b)(y - b), \] and \(f(x, y) \approx L(x, y)\) near \((a, b)\), with error \(O(\|(x - a, y - b)\|^2)\). Existence of \(f_x\), \(f_y\) alone does not guarantee differentiability; continuity of \(f_x\), \(f_y\) near \((a, b)\) does. The differential \(dz = f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy\) estimates the change \(\Delta z\). If the inputs are measured with errors at most \(|dx|\), \(|dy|\), then \(|\Delta z| \lesssim |f_x||dx| + |f_y||dy|\). In relative terms the coefficients are condition numbers, exactly as in meeting 2.

Examples

Example 1. Linearize \(f(x, y) = \sqrt{1 + xy}\) at \((3, 1)\), and estimate \(f(3.1, 0.9)\).

Answer

\(f(3, 1) = 2\); \(f_x = y/(2f) = 0.25\), \(f_y = x/(2f) = 0.75\). \(L(x, y) = 2 + 0.25(x - 3) + 0.75(y - 1)\), so \(L(3.1, 0.9) = 2 + 0.025 - 0.075 = 1.95\). Exact: \(\sqrt{3.79} = 1.94679\), error \(3.2\times10^{-3}\), of the size of the step squared.

Example 2. A pendulum gives \(g = 4\pi^2L/T^2\). With \(L = 1.00 \pm 0.01\) m and \(T = 2.00 \pm 0.02\) s, estimate the largest relative error in \(g\).

Answer

\(\dfrac{dg}{g} = \dfrac{dL}{L} - 2\dfrac{dT}{T}\), so \(\left|\dfrac{\Delta g}{g}\right| \lesssim 1\% + 2\cdot1\% = 3\%\): \(g = 9.87 \pm 0.30\) m/s². The condition numbers are 1 for \(L\) and 2 for \(T\). Measuring \(T\) more precisely pays twice as much as measuring \(L\).

2. The Jacobian matrix (Heath §5.6.2)

For a vector function \(\mathbf F(\mathbf x) = (F_1(\mathbf x), \ldots, F_m(\mathbf x))\) of \(\mathbf x \in \mathbb R^n\), the Jacobian is the \(m\times n\) matrix of first partials, one row per component (each row is a gradient): \[ J(\mathbf x) = \begin{bmatrix} \partial F_i/\partial x_j \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf F(\mathbf x + \mathbf s) \approx \mathbf F(\mathbf x) + J(\mathbf x)\,\mathbf s . \] This is the linearization of every component at once. Newton's method for \(\mathbf F(\mathbf x) = \mathbf 0\) sets the right side to zero and solves \(J\mathbf s = -\mathbf F\) (meeting 19). A Jacobian can be approximated column by column with finite differences, \(n + 1\) evaluations of \(\mathbf F\) (meeting 5).

Example

Example 3. For \(\mathbf F(x, y) = (x^2 - y,\ xy + 1)\), find \(J(1, 2)\) and use it to approximate \(\mathbf F(1.1, 1.9)\).

Answer

\(J = \begin{bmatrix} 2x & -1 \\ y & x \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) at \((1, 2)\); \(\mathbf F(1, 2) = (-1, 3)\). With \(\mathbf s = (0.1, -0.1)\): \(J\mathbf s = (0.3, 0.1)\), so \(\mathbf F(1.1, 1.9) \approx (-0.7, 3.1)\). Exact: \((-0.69, 3.09)\).

3. Second-order Taylor expansion

With the gradient \(\nabla f\) and the Hessian \(H_f = [\partial^2 f/\partial x_i\partial x_j]\) (symmetric, by Clairaut): \[ f(\mathbf x + \mathbf s) = f(\mathbf x) + \nabla f(\mathbf x)^T\mathbf s + \tfrac12\mathbf s^TH_f(\mathbf x)\,\mathbf s + O(\|\mathbf s\|^3) . \] The linear model has error \(O(\|\mathbf s\|^2)\), the quadratic model \(O(\|\mathbf s\|^3)\). Minimizing the quadratic model gives Newton's step for optimization, \(H_f\mathbf s = -\nabla f\) (meeting 22). At a critical point the Hessian term decides the type (meeting 20).

Example

Example 4. For \(f(x, y) = e^{x - y}\) at \((0, 0)\), write the linear and quadratic models, and compare their errors at \((h, h/2)\) for \(h = 0.2, 0.1, 0.05\).

Answer

\(\nabla f = (1, -1)\), \(H_f = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\): \(L = 1 + (x - y)\), \(Q = 1 + (x - y) + \tfrac12(x - y)^2\). Errors of \(L\): \(5.2\times10^{-3}\), \(1.27\times10^{-3}\), \(3.2\times10^{-4}\) (factor 4 per halving: second order). Errors of \(Q\): \(1.7\times10^{-4}\), \(2.1\times10^{-5}\), \(2.6\times10^{-6}\) (factor 8: third order). The figure shows the same slopes.

  2. Why does the existence of \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) not imply that \(f\) has a tangent plane? What extra condition suffices?
  3. Mass and speed are measured with relative errors of 0.5% and 1%. Estimate the relative error of the kinetic energy \(E = \tfrac12mv^2\).
  4. Write the Jacobian of \(\mathbf F(x, y, z) = (xyz,\ x^2 - z,\ e^y)\). What shape is it?
  5. How would you check a hand-computed Jacobian numerically?
  6. The error of a linear model is \(4\times10^{-3}\) at step \(0.1\). Predict it at step \(0.05\). What about the quadratic model?
  7. Write the quadratic Taylor model of \(f(x, y) = \cos x + y^2\) at \((0, 0)\).
