1. Lagrange multipliers (Stewart §14.8, Heath §6.2.3)

To minimize \(f(\mathbf x)\) subject to \(g(\mathbf x) = 0\): at a constrained extremum, \(f\) cannot change to first order along the constraint. So \(\nabla f\) is perpendicular to the constraint curve, like \(\nabla g\), and the level curve of \(f\) touches the constraint tangentially (figure): \[ \nabla f(\mathbf x^*) = \lambda\nabla g(\mathbf x^*), \qquad g(\mathbf x^*) = 0 \qquad (n + 1 \text{ equations in } \mathbf x, \lambda). \] With \(m\) constraints, \(\nabla f = \sum_i\lambda_i\nabla g_i\), assuming the \(\nabla g_i\) are linearly independent. Heath writes the same conditions with the Lagrangian \(\mathcal L(\mathbf x, \boldsymbol\lambda) = f(\mathbf x) + \boldsymbol\lambda^T\mathbf g(\mathbf x)\) and \(\nabla\mathcal L = \mathbf 0\), i.e. \(\nabla f + J_{\mathbf g}^T\boldsymbol\lambda = \mathbf 0\). His \(\lambda\) has the opposite sign to Stewart's; the minimizer is the same.

Meaning of \(\lambda\) (Stewart's sign): if the constraint becomes \(g(\mathbf x) = c\), the optimal value changes at the rate \(df^*/dc = \lambda\). Economists call it a shadow price.

Examples

Example 1. Minimize \(f(x, y) = x^2 + 2y^2\) subject to \(x + y = 3\). Then estimate the minimum for \(x + y = 3.1\) from \(\lambda\).

Answer

\((2x, 4y) = \lambda(1, 1)\) gives \(x = 2y\); with \(x + y = 3\): \(y = 1\), \(x = 2\), \(\lambda = 4\), \(f^* = 6\). For \(x + y = 3.1\): \(f^* \approx 6 + 4(0.1) = 6.4\). Exact: \(6.4067\). (Second-order check: \(f\) is convex and the constraint linear, so this is the global minimum.)

Example 2. Find the points of the hyperbola \(xy = 4\) closest to the origin (minimize \(x^2 + y^2\)).

Answer

\((2x, 2y) = \lambda(y, x)\). Multiplying, \(2x^2 = \lambda xy = 2y^2\), so \(y = \pm x\); with \(xy = 4\), \(y = x = \pm2\). Then \(\lambda = 2\), and the points \((2, 2)\), \((-2, -2)\) at distance \(\sqrt8\).

2. The KKT system and second-order conditions (Heath §6.2.3)

The critical points of \(\mathcal L\) solve a system of \(n + m\) equations. Its Jacobian is the symmetric KKT matrix \[ \begin{bmatrix} B & J_{\mathbf g}^T \\ J_{\mathbf g} & O \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = H_f + \sum_i\lambda_iH_{g_i} , \] which is indefinite even at a minimum: a constrained minimum is a saddle point of \(\mathcal L\), not a minimum. So Cholesky does not apply, and the second-order test looks only at directions along the constraint. \(\mathbf x^*\) is a constrained minimum if \(\mathbf s^TB\mathbf s > 0\) for all \(\mathbf s \ne \mathbf 0\) with \(J_{\mathbf g}\mathbf s = \mathbf 0\) (projected Hessian \(Z^TBZ\) positive definite). For a quadratic \(f\) and linear constraints the KKT system is linear: one solve, no iteration.

Inequality constraints \(h_i(\mathbf x) \le 0\) (Karush–Kuhn–Tucker): \(\nabla f + \sum\lambda_i\nabla h_i = \mathbf 0\) with \(\lambda_i \ge 0\) and complementarity \(\lambda_ih_i(\mathbf x^*) = 0\). Each constraint is either active (\(h_i = 0\)) or has \(\lambda_i = 0\) and may be ignored.

Example

Example 3. Write Example 1 as a KKT system with Heath's sign, solve it, and find the eigenvalues of the KKT matrix. What changes if the constraint is \(x + y \ge 3\)? Or \(x + y \ge -1\)?

Answer

\(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ \lambda \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\) gives \((x, y, \lambda) = (2, 1, -4)\): Heath's \(\lambda\) is minus Stewart's. Eigenvalues: \(-0.60\), \(2.34\), \(4.26\), so the matrix is indefinite. With \(x + y \ge 3\) the unconstrained minimizer \((0, 0)\) is infeasible, the constraint is active, and the answer is unchanged. With \(x + y \ge -1\), \((0, 0)\) is feasible, the constraint is inactive (multiplier 0), and the minimum is \(f = 0\).

3. Sequential quadratic programming (Heath §6.7.1)

Apply Newton's method (meeting 19) to \(\nabla\mathcal L(\mathbf x, \boldsymbol\lambda) = \mathbf 0\). Each step solves the KKT system \[ \begin{bmatrix} B(\mathbf x_k, \boldsymbol\lambda_k) & J_{\mathbf g}^T(\mathbf x_k) \\ J_{\mathbf g}(\mathbf x_k) & O \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf s_k \\ \boldsymbol\delta_k \end{bmatrix} = -\begin{bmatrix} \nabla f(\mathbf x_k) + J_{\mathbf g}^T(\mathbf x_k)\boldsymbol\lambda_k \\ \mathbf g(\mathbf x_k) \end{bmatrix}, \] which is also the optimality condition of a quadratic model of \(f\) subject to the linearized constraints: hence "sequential quadratic programming". Near a solution it converges quadratically. Practical codes add a line search on a merit function and quasi-Newton approximations of \(B\). scipy.optimize.minimize(method="SLSQP") and "trust-constr" implement such methods.

Example

Example 4. Run Newton's method on \(\nabla\mathcal L = \mathbf 0\) for Example 2 (\(\mathcal L = x^2 + y^2 + \lambda(xy - 4)\)) from \((x, y, \lambda) = (3, 1, -1)\).

Answer

Iterates: \((1.346, 1.885, -1.808)\), \((2.0375, 2.0035, -2.0482)\), \((2.00023, 1.99983, -2.00049)\), then \((2, 2, -2)\) to 8 and then 16 digits. Norms of \(\nabla\mathcal L\): \(5.2, 2.1, 0.19, 1.8\times10^{-3}, 1.5\times10^{-7}, 8\times10^{-16}\), quadratic convergence. (\(\lambda = -2\) is Stewart's \(+2\).)

4. Penalty and barrier methods (Heath §6.7.2)

Replace the constrained problem by unconstrained ones: \[ \min_{\mathbf x}\ \phi_\rho(\mathbf x) = f(\mathbf x) + \tfrac{\rho}{2}\,\mathbf g(\mathbf x)^T\mathbf g(\mathbf x), \qquad \rho \to \infty . \] The minimizers \(\mathbf x_\rho\) approach \(\mathbf x^*\) with error \(O(1/\rho)\), and \(\rho\,\mathbf g(\mathbf x_\rho) \to \boldsymbol\lambda\) (Heath's sign). The catch: the Hessian of \(\phi_\rho\) has condition number growing like \(\rho\), so the subproblems become ill conditioned. In practice \(\rho\) is increased gradually, warm-starting each solve from the last one. Augmented Lagrangian methods avoid needing \(\rho \to \infty\). Barrier methods handle inequalities from inside the feasible region, e.g. adding \(-\mu\sum\ln(-h_i(\mathbf x))\) with \(\mu \to 0\) (interior-point methods).

Example

Example 5. Penalty method for Example 1: \(\phi_\rho = x^2 + 2y^2 + \tfrac\rho2(x + y - 3)^2\). Find \(\mathbf x_\rho\), its error and \(\operatorname{cond}(H_{\phi_\rho})\) for \(\rho = 1, 10, 100, 1000\).

Answer

\(\nabla\phi_\rho = \mathbf 0\) is the linear system \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 + \rho & \rho \\ \rho & 4 + \rho \end{bmatrix}\mathbf x = 3\rho\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\).

\(\rho\)\(\mathbf x_\rho\)\(\|\mathbf x_\rho - \mathbf x^*\|\)\(\operatorname{cond}(H)\)\(\rho\,g(\mathbf x_\rho)\)
1(0.857, 0.429)1.282.1−1.71
10(1.765, 0.882)0.267.8−3.53
100(1.974, 0.987)0.02968−3.95

The error falls like \(3/\rho\) while the condition number grows like \(0.67\rho\); \(\rho\,g\) tends to \(\lambda = -4\).
