1. The chain rule (Stewart §14.5)

If \(z = f(u, v)\) with \(u = u(x, y)\), \(v = v(x, y)\), then \[ \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} . \] In matrix form, for any composition \(\mathbf h = \mathbf f\circ\mathbf g\): \(J_{\mathbf h}(\mathbf x) = J_{\mathbf f}(\mathbf g(\mathbf x))\,J_{\mathbf g}(\mathbf x)\). The derivative of a composition is the product of the derivatives, and the linearization of a composition is the composition of linearizations. This is the rule that automatic differentiation and back-propagation in neural networks apply step by step (meeting 28). Implicit differentiation: if \(F(x, y) = 0\) defines \(y(x)\), then \(dy/dx = -F_x/F_y\).

Example

Example 1. \(z = u^2v\) with \(u = r\cos\theta\), \(v = r\sin\theta\). Find \(\partial z/\partial r\) and \(\partial z/\partial\theta\) at \((r, \theta) = (2, \pi/4)\) in matrix form.

Answer

At \((2, \pi/4)\), \(u = v = \sqrt2\). \([z_u\ z_v] = [2uv\ \ u^2] = [4\ \ 2]\). \(J_{\mathbf g} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.7071 & -1.4142 \\ 0.7071 & 1.4142 \end{bmatrix}\). Product: \([z_r\ z_\theta] = [4.2426\ \ {-2.8284}]\), i.e. \(3\sqrt2\) and \(-2\sqrt2\). Check: \(z = r^3\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\), so \(z_r = 3r^2\cos^2\theta\sin\theta = 3\sqrt2\).

2. Directional derivatives and the gradient (Stewart §14.6)

The gradient \(\nabla f = (f_x, f_y)\) (or \((f_{x_1}, \ldots, f_{x_n})\)) gives the rate of change in any direction of a unit vector \(\mathbf u\): \[ D_{\mathbf u}f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf u = \|\nabla f\|\cos\theta . \] So \(f\) increases fastest in the direction of \(\nabla f\), at rate \(\|\nabla f\|\); decreases fastest along \(-\nabla f\); and does not change to first order along directions perpendicular to \(\nabla f\), which are tangent to the level curve. Hence \(\nabla f\) is perpendicular to the level curve through the point (figure), and in 3-D to the level surface: the tangent plane to \(F(x, y, z) = k\) has normal \(\nabla F\). Steepest descent (meeting 22) moves along \(-\nabla f\), and the first-order optimality condition is \(\nabla f = \mathbf 0\) (meeting 20).

Examples

Example 2. For \(f(x, y) = x^2y - y^3\) at \((1, 2)\): find \(\nabla f\), the rate of change toward \((4, 6)\), the maximum rate of change, and the tangent line to the level curve through \((1, 2)\).

Answer

\(\nabla f = (2xy, x^2 - 3y^2) = (4, -11)\). Toward \((4, 6)\): \(\mathbf u = (3, 4)/5\), so \(D_{\mathbf u}f = (12 - 44)/5 = -6.4\): \(f\) decreases in that direction. Maximum rate \(\|\nabla f\| = \sqrt{137} = 11.70\), along \((4, -11)/\sqrt{137}\). The level curve \(f = -6\) has normal \((4, -11)\), so its tangent line is \(4(x - 1) - 11(y - 2) = 0\).

Example 3. Check \(\nabla f(1, 2)\) of Example 2 with centered differences, \(h = 10^{-5}\).

Answer

\(\bigl(f(1 + h, 2) - f(1 - h, 2)\bigr)/(2h) = 4.0000000000\) and \(\bigl(f(1, 2 + h) - f(1, 2 - h)\bigr)/(2h) = -11.0000000002\); errors \(3\times10^{-11}\) and \(2\times10^{-10}\). A gradient check like this catches nearly every sign or algebra mistake before an optimizer silently goes wrong.

Example 4. Take one steepest-descent step \(\mathbf x_1 = \mathbf x_0 - \alpha\nabla f(\mathbf x_0)\) with \(\alpha = 0.1\) for \(f(x, y) = x^2 + 4y^2\) from \(\mathbf x_0 = (2, 1)\).

Answer

\(\nabla f = (2x, 8y) = (4, 8)\), so \(\mathbf x_1 = (2 - 0.4, 1 - 0.8) = (1.6, 0.2)\), and \(f\) drops from 8 to 2.72. The step is perpendicular to the level ellipse through \((2, 1)\), not aimed at the minimum \((0, 0)\). That is why steepest descent zigzags on elongated contours (meeting 22).

5. Try it, and review questions

Interactive: Gradient and directional derivatives. Lab 4 draws gradient fields on contour plots and checks the chain rule numerically.

  1. Write the chain rule for \(z = f(x, y)\), \(x = x(t)\), \(y = y(t)\) as a product of a \(1\times2\) and a \(2\times1\) matrix.
  2. Use implicit differentiation to find \(dy/dx\) on the curve \(x^3 + y^3 = 9\) at \((1, 2)\).
  3. Why is \(\nabla f\) perpendicular to the level curve of \(f\)? Prove it with the chain rule.
  4. In which directions is \(D_{\mathbf u}f = 0\)? How many such directions are there in 2-D, and in 3-D?
  5. Find the tangent plane to the surface \(x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 1\) at \((1, 1, 1)\).
  6. Why is a step along \(-\nabla f\) a descent step for small enough \(\alpha\)? Why may it be a poor step for elongated contours?
  7. How would you test whether a function you wrote returns the correct gradient?
